There's about an inch of skin at your collarbone that decides whether a necklace is flattering you or working against you. Land on it, and a plain sweater starts reading like money. Land two inches lower, and that same necklace drags your chin and your jawline down with it. We're taught to shop for the pendant and the stones. But the pendant is almost never the problem. From a few feet away, the first one does something none of the others quite manage. It softens the line of the jaw without covering anything up. Number one is pearls, worn at sixteen to eighteen inches.

Not the long rope knotted at the waist. A single strand sitting right at the collarbone, or one modern pearl on a fine chain at the same height, which is the version I reach for on an ordinary Tuesday.

Here's why pearls keep earning their place after sixty. A pearl doesn't bounce light back at you the way a polished metal does. It scatters it. The light lands on the pearl, breaks up, and comes back at your face soft and even, and soft even light is the kindest thing you can put near mature skin. It's the same reason a lamp with a fabric shade flatters a room and a bare bulb doesn't.

Pearls also bridge. If you've spent years deciding whether you're a gold woman or a silver woman, pearls sit politely in the middle and go with both. The mistake with pearls is saving them. The good strand lives in a box, wrapped in tissue, waiting for a wedding that keeps not happening. Wear it with a navy crewneck and flat loafers to the grocery store. One note on size, because it matters more than the price does. Very tiny pearls, the sort of seed strand a young girl gets for a graduation, tend to look thin on a grown woman and can read a little dated. A pearl about the size of a pencil eraser, give or take, sits far better against an adult neckline and carries more light with it.

Picture a family lunch. Camel trousers, an ivory knit, flat loafers, and one strand of pearls at the collarbone. Nobody in that room can tell you what anything cost, and every one of them can tell you that you look lovely.

Pair them with a crew neck or a shallow V, and let the strand sit on skin rather than on top of the sweater. Against skin it lifts. On top of a knit it's just decoration.

Simple, warm, and impossible to argue with.

At conversation distance, the second one barely registers as jewelry, and that's the whole point of it. Number two is a fine gold chain with one small pendant. I think of it as the layer of light.

We're talking thin, thin enough that you notice the shine before you notice the chain, sitting in the little hollow just below the throat with one small drop on it. A tiny disc, a knot, a single stone, nothing that announces itself.

What that thin line of gold actually does is put a moving highlight right under your chin. Skin loses some of its natural glow over the years, and the eye reads light near the face as health, as rest, as a good night's sleep you may or may not have had. A fine chain borrows that back for you. It catches the light every time you turn your head, so it's never a flat piece sitting there.

And a small pendant gives the eye a place to stop. A bare chain is pretty. A bare chain with one small weight on it looks intentional, and intentional is most of what expensive means.

Picture a crisp white shirt with the top two buttons open, sleeves pushed up, a fine gold chain at the throat and nothing else on your neck at all. Errands, lunch, a meeting with the bank.

If you want a second chain with it, and plenty of women do, keep it in the same metal and make it clearly shorter or clearly longer, never the same length. Two fine chains an inch apart will spend the day knotted together no matter what you do.

Put it with an open collar or an unbuttoned shirt, and if you're wearing a jacket, let the chain sit inside the collar rather than over it. Barely there, and there's no improving on it.

Walking toward someone, the third one doesn't read as a necklace at all. It reads as a line. Number three is the long chain, twenty-eight to thirty-two inches.

That length hangs well past the collarbone, down onto the ribs or just below. And a long unbroken vertical line does one reliable thing to a body: it makes it look longer. The eye travels down the chain instead of stopping at the widest point of your middle. Nothing about you changed. The eye just took a different route.

This is the length I want you reaching for if your torso is short, if your middle is fuller than it used to be, or if a bust you've had your whole life now sits a little lower than it did. It breaks up a solid block of fabric and gives the front of you some structure.

It's also the answer to the turtleneck problem. A turtleneck is one of the best things a woman over sixty can own in cold weather, and it swallows every short necklace you put on it. From a few feet away, the fourth one reads as a single clean line at the base of the throat, and everything you've put on underneath it suddenly looks deliberate. Number four is the slim collar, sometimes called a torque.

What it does that a chain can't is hold a shape. A chain follows your body and pools where it wants to. A collar sits exactly where the maker put it, and that stillness is what reads as designer even when the piece cost very little. It also keeps the eye at the very highest point of your chest, which is useful under a crew neck, where a shorter chain has nowhere flattering to land.

Width is the decision to get right. A slim collar, no thicker than a piece of cord, looks modern and light and flatters nearly everyone who wants to wear one. A wide one starts to look like armor and it will shorten the neck in a photograph. When in doubt, go thinner than feels brave.

This is also the piece that rewards short hair. If you wear a bob, a pixie, or your hair up, the whole neck and shoulder line is already on show, and a collar finishes it the way a good frame finishes a picture. With longer hair down, it mostly hides.

The collar goes with a crew neck, a boat neck, or bare shoulders in summer. Clean, modern, and not the least bit fussy.

Before we turn to the three that are aging you, I want to say something about money, because I know what happens the moment a woman hears the word expensive.

Good freshwater pearls run somewhere around forty to a hundred and twenty dollars for a strand. A gold-filled chain, which is a real layer of gold bonded over a base metal and holds up for years of daily wear, sits around thirty to eighty. A solid fourteen karat chain runs a hundred and fifty to four hundred, and that one you'll still have in twenty years, and so will your daughter.

And you don't have to pay the top of any of those ranges. End-of-season racks, the good consignment shop in the nicer part of town, an estate sale on a Saturday morning. Real pearls and solid gold turn up second hand constantly, because they outlive the women who bought them and the daughters don't always want them.

None of that is what makes a necklace look expensive. Scale and finish are. A well-proportioned piece in a bright, unworn finish will beat a heavy one that cost three times as much, every time, at every distance a person is ever going to see you from. Which brings us neatly to what goes wrong.

Now the other side of the list. And I'll say what I always say about mistakes. Every one of these is something nearly all of us have done, my own jewelry box included, and not one of them is about your body.

From across a room, the first mistake reads as a dark block sitting on your chest. Number one is chunky black or very dark beads.

You know the piece. Big matte beads, jet or dark wood or dark resin, worn as a statement, usually bought because it looked bold and modern on the shop counter.

Here's what it does. Those beads absorb light instead of returning it, so you've placed a shadow across the brightest part of your upper body. And there are already soft shadows under the chin and along the jaw on all of us after a certain point. The dark necklace echoes them. The eye reads two shadows in the same region and treats them as one, so the face looks tired and the jawline looks softer than it actually is. A heavy dark piece close to the throat will add years to a photograph that nothing else in the outfit would have.

There's a quick way to prove this to yourself, and it's slightly brutal. Take a photograph of yourself in the dark necklace, then take the same photograph with nothing on your neck at all, same light, same top, one minute apart. Look at the two side by side on your phone. It's usually the jaw where you see it first.

The swap is not a smaller necklace. Keep the scale if you love a statement piece. Change the finish. The same size beads in ivory, in oat, in a warm coral, in mother of pearl, in a bright polished metal, all of that hands light upward instead of eating it. If you can't part with the dark piece, at least stop wearing it on a dark top. Dark on dark doubles the effect. Put it on ivory or camel and let it be contrast instead of a shadow.

Heavy where you most need light.

From a few feet away, the second mistake doesn't look like jewelry. It looks like something that's been in a drawer too long. Number two is the thick tarnished costume chain.

And I want to be careful here, because the piece itself may be perfectly good. A chunky chain in a clean bright finish can look wonderful on a woman over sixty. It's the tarnish doing the damage.

Tarnish turns gold-tone dull and brownish and turns silver-tone gray and cloudy. Dullness reads as neglect, every time, whatever the piece originally cost. And fair or not, the eye transfers that straight onto everything else you have on. The cashmere stops looking like cashmere. You can be beautifully put together from the shoes up and undone by four inches of dull metal at your throat.

Look at the piece you wear most in daylight, near a window. Look at where it rubs, at the back of the links, at the edges of a clasp. If the gold tone has worn through to a gray or coppery base underneath, that one is finished and it's doing you no favors. Retire it kindly.

This is also the argument for owning less and owning better. Plated jewelry has a thin skin of gold over a cheaper metal underneath, and that skin wears through at the exact points that rub, which is why the chain at the back of your neck goes first. Gold-filled is a thicker layer and lasts far longer. Solid gold has nothing to wear through at all. One good chain you wear constantly will always beat a drawer of plated ones going dull in the dark.

For everything still good: a soft jewelry cloth, thirty seconds, once a week. And store pieces in a closed bag or a lined box rather than open on the dresser, because air is what does it. Keep your metals matched while you're at it. Gold with gold, silver with silver, and pearls when you can't decide.

A clean finish will do more for you than a bigger piece ever will.

And at any distance at all, the last mistake doesn't read as a piece of jewelry. It reads as busy. Number three is several mismatched layers over a busy print.

Three chains at three lengths, one gold, one silver, one beaded, worn over a floral blouse.

Layering is not the problem. Layering is lovely, and it's having a real moment. The problem is that a printed blouse is already giving the eye a dozen lines to follow. Add three more that don't match each other and the eye has nowhere to land. It bounces around your chest and gives up, and that bouncing is exactly what a viewer registers as cheap, even when every single piece on you is real.

There's a related version of this that's even more common. Three chains all cut to nearly the same length, so they tangle and twist into one confused rope all day, and you spend the afternoon separating them at red lights.

And remember that your earrings are in this count as well. A printed blouse, three necklaces, and a pair of chandelier earrings is four things competing inside the same twelve inches, all of it right beside your face. Something has to give way, and it should never be your face.

So here's the rule I follow. Over a print, one piece. That's it. Let the blouse be the pattern and let one clean pendant be the jewelry. If you want to layer, layer over a solid, keep every chain in the same metal, and space the lengths properly, sixteen, twenty, twenty-four, so there's clear skin or fabric between each one. Then it looks composed instead of collected.

One piece, one metal, one place for the eye to land.

So, two things run underneath this entire list, and once you have them you don't need the list anymore.

The first is where the necklace ends. A crew neck or a shallow V wants the piece at the collarbone, right around sixteen to eighteen inches. A turtleneck or a mock neck wants it below the neckline entirely, twenty-eight and up, or it vanishes into the knit. An open collar wants it high, in the notch at the base of the throat. And a print wants one piece and nothing else. Match the length to the neckline and half your jewelry problems disappear on their own.

The second is light. Hold the piece up near your face by a window and just look at what it gives back. Does it brighten the skin under your chin, or does it put a shadow there? Pearls, ivory, polished gold and silver, mother of pearl, any warm bright stone, all of those hand light upward. Matte black, dull tarnish, heavy dark wood, all of those take it away. That's the entire test, and it takes four seconds.

From there it's personal. Warm undertones, the ones who look best in cream and camel and coral, generally want gold near the face. Cool undertones, the ones who look best in true white and charcoal and blue-reds, generally want silver. If you've never been sure which you are, pearls settle it for you and always have.

And if you do one thing tonight, do this. Take out the necklace you reach for most, put it on with what you're actually planning to wear tomorrow, and look at where it stops. Don't study the necklace itself. Study the spot where it ends. That's usually the whole answer.
