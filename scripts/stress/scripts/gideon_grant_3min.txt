There is a man standing in the greeting card aisle of a pharmacy on a Tuesday morning, buying his third sympathy card since spring, and doing a kind of arithmetic nobody ever taught him. He is counting backward. He is sixty-four. His father made it to eighty-one. And somewhere between the sympathy cards and the cards for new grandbabies, he has quietly worked out that if the family average holds, he has about seventeen summers left.

Seventeen. He pays, he walks out to the parking lot, and the number rides home with him in the passenger seat. Maybe you have done that arithmetic. Most people past sixty have — usually in silence, usually in some ordinary place, a waiting room, a church pew, the kitchen table at two in the morning when the house is too quiet.

And the math always presents itself as the problem. Fewer years ahead than behind, and a clock that was background noise at thirty is now the loudest thing in the room. My name is Gideon Grant, and this channel is for men and women in their fifties, sixties, and beyond — people carrying real questions about money, grief, difficult people, and what the remaining years are actually for.

Today, with the help of a Roman who wrote the sharpest essay on time ever put to paper, I want to show you that the arithmetic was never your problem. Your problem is theft, and by the end of this video you will be able to name all seven of the thieves. Two thousand years ago, a man named Lucius Annaeus Seneca wrote a short, furious, loving essay to a man named Paulinus, the official in charge of Rome’s grain supply — busy, important, and worn down to the rims.

Seneca was no monk on a hillside. He was one of the richest men in the empire, an advisor standing one step from the throne, buried under exactly the kind of obligations we are about to talk about — and a man who, years later, would be ordered to end his own life by the very emperor he had served.

When a man like that sits down to write about time, I pull my chair in closer. The essay is called On the Shortness of Life, and it opens by picking a fight with its own title. Everyone complains that life is too short, Seneca says. The farmer complains, the senator complains, the old complain, and the young are already rehearsing the complaint.

Then he turns the whole thing over in a single motion: life is not short.
