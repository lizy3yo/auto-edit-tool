Out of 10 Japanese woodworking projects you can build from cheap box-store lumber, the one that paid me best for my time came out of a coffee can of burnt scraps I almost threw in the trash. But the one I was proudest of, a lattice panel that ate 6 hours of my Saturday, finished dead last at about $14 an hour.

I'm Hank Hardwood, and this one's for anybody standing in a garage with a saw, a drill, and a stack of sandpaper, wondering if that clean Japanese look is really worth anything on a market table.

Start with the one that lost. Kumiko is the Japanese craft of fitting thin strips of wood together into patterns, the kind of lattice you see in Japanese sliding doors. Mine was a plain grid about a foot square, made from pine screen molding at about $3 a stick. Every place two strips cross needs a notch cut halfway through each one, and every notch has to be dead on or the whole grid twists out of square. I figured 2 hours. It took 6. It sells for $90 to $130, but once you take out the lumber, that's about $14 for every hour I stood at the bench.

Now the winner. Charred cedar incense holders, made from the scraps in that coffee can. After a long day of burning planters and coasters, that can was packed with burnt ends and corners, and it was sitting right next to the trash bin. I was about to dump the whole thing when I thought, why not drill a hole in one and see?

Square up the scrap with a couple of saw cuts, give it a quick brush, drill the hole about halfway through at a slight angle, oil it, and put a felt dot on the bottom. That's the whole build. It takes 12 minutes apiece, and the cost is about 40 cents. Every one comes out a little different, and buyers liked picking out their favorite. Now, charred wood isn't fireproof, so a lit stick never gets left alone in a room.

They sell for $16 to $22, and I sold every one I had in a single afternoon. 40 cents and 12 minutes selling for around $19 comes out to about $93 an hour. That's more than six times what the lattice panel paid me, out of a can of scraps I nearly threw away.

So the Japanese look people paid me for wasn't the precision work. It was the fire, because once a piece is charred and brushed, a crooked saw line just looks like character.
