Out of 10 crochet projects I've made from the cheapest worsted-weight yarn the craft store sells, the one that paid me best for my time came out of a bag of leftover scraps I'd have been ashamed to show anybody. But the one I was proudest of, a lacy afghan that ate up most of two weeks in my rocking chair, finished dead last at about a dollar-fifty an hour. So we're counting all of them down, worst to best, with what each one cost me, how long it took, and what folks actually handed over for it.

I'm Granny Mae, and this one's for anybody sitting at a kitchen table with a hook, a skein of yarn, and a stack of stitch books, wondering if all that handwork is really worth anything on a bazaar table. Folks will tell you that to make a dime crocheting you need fancy alpaca and merino and yarn that costs more than a good roast, and after this little experiment of mine, I can tell you exactly where that advice would've cost me money.

Number ten. The lace shell afghan, the one I was proudest of.

A shell-stitch afghan is one of those big lacy throws with the fans of stitches marching across it in rows, the kind you see folded over the back of the good couch in the front parlor, and a real lace afghan takes a patient soul and a whole lot of counting. I'm not going to pretend mine belonged in a magazine. What I made was a throw about four feet by five, a plain shell pattern with a simple border, the beginner cousin of the fancy pineapple lace the church ladies show off.

The lace shell eats yarn like nothing else on this list, because all those open fans need row after row after row, and I went through eight skeins at about $3 apiece before I was done. Everywhere the pattern shells out, you have to count your stitches dead on, or the whole thing leans off to one side, and I frogged more rows than I'd want anybody to see me pull out. I sat with that afghan through two weeks of evening television, my hook going while the shows came and went.

This is where the whole thing went sideways on me. I'd figured a week, maybe. The little tally I keep in the margin of my calendar says I put close to fifty hours into that throw, because every shell has to match its neighbor or the edges wave like a flag in the wind. Materials came to about $26 with the yarn and a bit for the border. It sells for $80 to $100, and it did draw folks over too, because nobody can walk past a big soft afghan without stopping to pet it. But once you take out the yarn, that's about a dollar-fifty for every hour I sat there hooking, and hourly pay is how I'm ranking this whole list, because it's the number that tells you whether your two weeks were worth the trouble.

So who buys it? Folks setting up a nice room, the kind who want one good handmade throw on the sofa and nothing store-bought about it, and they'll pay real money for a piece like that on a bare arm of a chair. That afghan did earn its keep another way, though. It pulled people right up to my table at the fall bazaar in the church hall, and more than one of them, once they'd stopped, walked off with a set of the little dishcloths you'll meet later. So the piece I'm proudest of finished stone last, beaten by things a beginner can turn out in an evening.

Number nine. The baby blanket, and it only beat the afghan by about a dollar an hour.

A baby blanket is a smaller throw, usually a soft simple stitch in a sweet color, the kind that ends up folded on the rail of a crib in a nursery down the hall. I made mine in a plain granny-square pattern, six skeins of the softest cheap acrylic I could find, because a baby blanket has to be gentle on new skin and it has to wash a hundred times without going stiff. The granny square is friendly to a beginner, you just keep going round and round, but a whole blanket of them still takes a good long while.

And I do mean the soft acrylic, none of the scratchy stuff. A blanket that feels rough is a blanket a young mama sets in the closet and never uses, so you spend a little more on the soft yarn even when you're pinching pennies everywhere else, and you tell your buyer it's machine-washable when you hand it over, because that's the first thing a tired mother wants to know.

The squares went together easy enough, but joining them is where I lost my patience, because you've got all those edges to line up and stitch together, and if you rush it you get puckers. What finally worked for me was blocking each square with a little steam before I joined a single one, laying them out flat on a towel on the kitchen table until they all sat square and true. That's an old trick and it works on the cheap yarn too. And if a seam ever pulls loose after somebody's washed it fifty times, they can bring it back and I'll close it up in two minutes, which is a nice thing to be able to tell a young family.

Counting just the yarn that went into one blanket, it came to about $18. It took me around twenty hours. They sell for $45 to $60, mostly to grandmothers buying ahead for a baby shower, or a neighbor two doors down expecting in the spring. Take your photo with the blanket folded soft over the side of a crib or a rocking chair, because that sweetness is what they're paying for. That comes out to about a dollar-thirty-five an hour, for the softest thing I made all season.

So the two biggest, coziest things I made are sitting right at the bottom of this list, and number one is still hiding in a bag of scraps under my sewing table.

Number eight. The market bag, which climbed about two dollars an hour past the baby blanket with hardly any yarn at all.

A market bag is one of those open, stretchy net bags folks carry to the farm stand for their tomatoes and their sweet corn, and the open mesh is what makes it stretch to hold a load. I used a tough cotton yarn instead of the soft acrylic, about two skeins' worth, because a bag has to haul groceries without giving out. The pattern is mostly big open loops, a chain and a few stitches over and over, so it works up quick once you get the rhythm of it, and there's no fussy counting to trip you up.

I made mine with a good sturdy handle worked right into the top, doubled over so it won't cut into a shoulder full of produce. Cotton yarn holds its shape better than acrylic for a bag like this, and it washes up nice when it gets a squashed berry in the bottom, which it always does eventually. Folks who buy these want something they can use every Saturday and toss in the wash, not something they have to baby.

The one place this bag can go wrong is the bottom, because if you don't work it tight enough down there, a heavy melon will stretch a hole right through it at the worst possible moment, in the middle of the parking lot. So I go down a hook size on the bottom rounds and pack those stitches in close, and I've never had one give out yet.

Materials ran about $8 for the cotton. It took me around three hours. It sells for $22 to $30 to folks who shop the farmers market on Main Street and want to quit hauling home those flimsy plastic sacks. That works out to about $4.60 an hour. Still, the next one pulled ahead the minute I stitched somebody's initial into it.

Number seven. The kitchen dishcloth, a plain little cotton square, and it only moved up this list once I started making them in sets.

A crochet dishcloth is just what it sounds like, a small square of cotton for washing up, no lace and no fuss, and that plain usefulness is exactly what sells it. Mine is a simple textured stitch that scrubs a pot without scratching it, worked from one corner out to a square. One skein of that cheap cotton makes three or four of them, so there's next to nothing tied up in each one. I round off with a little single-crochet edge so they lie flat on the edge of the sink and don't curl up.

These cotton cloths hold up to real washing, hot water and all, so they get better with use instead of falling apart. The plain ones sold fine one at a time. But once I started stacking three together in a bundle and tying them with a bit of ribbon, they went for the top of the range every time, and they went first, because a bundle looks like a gift a body can grab on the way out the door. I set them out in a basket right at the front of my table at the craft fair, and they emptied faster than anything.

Somebody asked me once to work an initial into a corner for her sister, and after that I took orders at the table and had them ready the next weekend, and nobody minded the little wait. Put a person's initial on a thing and the price goes up, and these dishcloths proved it plain as day.

A bundle costs me about $2 in cotton and ribbon, it takes about an hour and a half for a set of three when I make them all at once, and a set sells for $12 to $15. That averages out right around $8 an hour. Even so, it lost out to a crowd I didn't know lived around here.

Number six. The baby booties, and they jumped almost three dollars an hour over the dishcloths.

Little crochet booties are those soft shoes for a baby's feet, the kind that end up in a gift bag at a shower or tucked beside a bassinet at the hospital when a new grandbaby arrives. They take almost no yarn at all, just the tail ends of a skein, so my cost is next to nothing. But they've got little parts, the sole and the sides and the strap, and getting them to match as a pair takes a careful hand.

The buyer is what surprised me. A young woman from the mothers' group over at the Methodist church bought four pairs at once, one for every baby due in her circle that year, and a week later her friends started calling the house. I had no idea there was such a crowd of new mamas around here, and those folks buy booties the way the rest of us buy stamps. After that first order I worked up a slightly bigger size for the older babies, and those went to the very same bunch.

The yarn cost me maybe 75 cents a pair, it takes about an hour for the two of them, and they go for $10 to $14. Keep your stitches small and tight so a curious baby can't work a toe through a gap. Call it $11 an hour. That's five projects down, and every one of them still took real sitting time. That's about to change, and my next-door neighbor got the first one right off my hook.

Number five. The simple crochet scarf, worked in that cheap self-striping yarn.

A scarf is about the friendliest thing a beginner can make and sell, just a long strip of nice warm stitches, and the self-striping yarn does all the fancy color-changing for you while you do nothing but keep hooking. One ball of that striping yarn, about $4, makes one good long scarf. I use a simple half-double stitch the whole way down, which gives a thick warm fabric that works up faster than you'd think.

The trick is the length and the width, because a scarf that's too skinny looks cheap and one that's too short won't wrap right, so I make mine good and long, long enough to loop once around and still hang down the front. A little fringe on each end, tied on with a hook, dresses it right up and hides where you started and stopped.

Before the striping yarn, a plain one-color scarf just looked like homework. With the stripes marching down it, it looks like something from a nice shop, and folks can see that from clear across the room. My neighbor across the fence saw the first one and bought it off me before I'd even woven in the ends.

One ball of yarn comes to about $4, it takes me around two hours counting the fringe, and they sell for $20 to $28 to anybody facing a cold winter, which around here is everybody. That pays about $10 an hour, and the yarn is doing most of the selling. The next one uses that same cheap yarn, plus a bit of stuffing.

Number four. The amigurumi bunny, a little stuffed animal, and it passed the scarf by a couple dollars an hour.

Amigurumi is the little stuffed critters worked in the round, and children go right to them, so a small toy sells itself on a table full of practical things. Mine is a bunny about six inches tall, worked in tight single crochet so the stuffing doesn't show through, from one skein of soft acrylic with a bit of polyfill inside. You work the body and head all in a spiral, then the ears and arms separate and sew them on.

The one thing you watch with a toy for a little one is the eyes, because a plastic safety eye is fine but a sewn-on button is a choking worry, so for the babies I embroider the eyes right on with a bit of dark yarn and tell the mama it's baby-safe. That care is exactly what a grandmother shopping for a one-year-old wants to hear.

Keeping your stitches tight is where a new hand struggles, because loose stitches leave gaps and the stuffing peeks through, so you go down a hook size from what the yarn calls for and pull each stitch snug. Give the bunny a little scarf of its own from a scrap, and it turns into a Christmas thing, which is where most of mine went in December.

The yarn and stuffing came to about $3, it took me around two hours, and they sell for $18 to $25, which lands at about $10.50 an hour. And yet what came next emptied my basket before anything else did.

Number three. The cotton washcloth sets for the bath, sold three to a bundle, and these were the first thing gone from my table.

One skein of that soft cotton gives you enough for a good bundle, with a little left over. Work each cloth in a pretty shell or waffle stitch so it looks nicer than the plain kitchen kind, a bit bigger and softer, meant for a person's face and not their frying pan. The step folks skip is the finishing. Give each one a neat even border so it lies flat and doesn't curl, then stack three, tie them with a ribbon, and tuck in a little wrapped bar of soap from the dollar store, and it looks like a spa gift before anybody even asks.

A bundle costs me about $3 counting the soap and ribbon, it takes about an hour and a half for a set of three when I batch them, and a set brings $20 to $26. They were gone before my first cup of coffee went cold at the spring craft fair. That's about $14 an hour, and they're the easiest yes on the whole table, because at that price a body can buy one for a gift without stopping to think it over. Every scrap I trimmed off went into a bag under my table, and by now that bag was getting full. The next one made more per piece than anything else I hooked.

Number two. The chunky basket, worked from doubled-up yarn, and no single piece I made brought in more money.

This one's made by holding two or three strands of that cheap yarn together and working them all at once with a big hook, which makes a thick stiff fabric that stands up on its own like a real basket. I work the flat bottom first, then bring the sides straight up, and I add little handles on two sides so a body can lift it. It uses a good bit of yarn, three or four skeins, but it works up fast because those doubled strands cover so much ground with every stitch.

Some folks think a basket ought to be woven from reeds and not crochet, and I love them for caring, but not one buyer ever asked. These sell to people who want a nice soft basket for the baby's toys in the nursery, or for the yarn in their own sewing room, or to set on the porch to hold the garden gloves. The stiffer you want it, the more strands you hold together, and I've made some so firm they'd hold a load of apples.

Three skeins run about $9, it takes me around three hours, and it sells for $35 to $48, mostly to folks who like things tidy and pretty both. That's about $12 an hour, which would've taken first place most any other week. But the jump from number two to number one is the biggest jump on this whole list.

Number one. Scrap-yarn coasters, made from the ends in that bag under my table.

By the time the scarves and the dishcloths and the baskets were all done, that bag was stuffed full of yarn ends and leftover bits, every color under the sun. It was the end of a long week of hooking, and that bag was sitting right next to the trash can. I was about to carry the whole thing out when I thought, well now, why not work a few of these ends into something? A coaster is just a small tight round that sits under a glass, and you can make one from a length of scrap too short for anything else.

Work a snug little round in single crochet till it's about four inches across, tuck in the two ends, and give it a firm press, and that's the whole thing. It takes me about ten minutes apiece, and the cost is nothing at all, because it's yarn I'd already paid for and set aside. Every one comes out a little different, because every scrap is a different color, and folks liked digging through the basket to pick out their favorites.

They sell for $4 to $6 apiece, or four for $15 as a set, and I sold every last one in a single afternoon to folks who wanted something cheerful and useful for the coffee table. Selling out that fast told me my price was a touch low, so the next batch went out closer to the top. When I added up my little tally, I did it twice, because a scrap of free yarn and ten minutes coming back around five dollars comes out to about $30 an hour. That's twenty times what the lace afghan paid me, out of a bag of ends I'd nearly thrown out.

Now look back at the top of this list. The coasters, the basket, the washcloth sets, the bunny, the scarf, none of them was the big fussy handwork I was so proud of, and the two slowest, hardest projects sat right at the bottom. What folks paid me best for wasn't the fancy lace and the endless counting. It was the small quick things they could grab up and use, because at those prices nobody's turning the piece over to hunt for a dropped stitch. My grandmother used to say a plain thing done neat will always outsell a fancy thing done slow, and a bag of scrap coasters proved her right all over again. And if that lace afghan grabs your heart the way it grabbed mine, hook it anyway, for your own good sofa, just don't count on it to buy next month's yarn.
