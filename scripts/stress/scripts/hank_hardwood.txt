Out of 10 Japanese woodworking projects you can build from cheap box-store lumber, the one that paid me best for my time came out of a coffee can of burnt scraps I almost threw in the trash. But the one I was proudest of, a lattice panel that ate 6 hours of my Saturday, finished dead last at about $14 an hour. Folks will tell you Japanese woodworking takes a master's hands and a wall full of fancy saws, and after this little experiment, I can tell you exactly where that advice cost me money.
 
Number ten. The kumiko-style lattice panel, the one I was proudest of.
 
Kumiko is the Japanese craft of fitting thin strips of wood together into patterns, the kind of lattice you see in Japanese sliding doors, and the real kumiko makers fit hundreds of tiny pieces into designs like the hemp leaf pattern without a single nail. I'm not going to pretend my grid belongs in the same room as theirs. What I made was a panel about a foot square, a plain grid of crossing strips inside a frame, the beginner cousin of the real thing.
 
The real craftsmen mill their own skinny strips with table saw jigs I don't own, and ripping thin strips on a regular table saw setup is exactly how people get hurt, so please don't try that with your fingers anywhere near the blade. I walked into the box store and bought pine screen molding instead, the skinny trim that comes already milled to size, six sticks at about $3 apiece. Everywhere two strips cross, you cut a notch halfway through each one so they slide together flush. I clamped the strips into a bundle with a stop block on the bench, sawed the notches through all of them at once with a cheap pull saw, and cleaned each notch out with a little chisel, both hands behind the edge the whole time.
 
This is where the whole thing went sideways on me. I figured 2 hours, tops. The chalk tally I keep on the garage wall says 360 minutes, 6 full hours, because every notch has to be dead on or the whole grid twists out of square, and I recut more strips than I'd want anybody to see. Materials came to about $26 with a one-by-two frame and glue. It sells for $90 to $130, and it drew a crowd too, because people can't walk past it without running their fingers across the grid. But once you take out the lumber, that's about $14 for every hour I stood at the bench, and hourly pay is how I'm ranking this whole list, because it's the number that decides whether your weekend was worth it.
 
So who buys it? Folks going for the Japandi look, that blend of Japanese and Scandinavian style with the low bed, the paper lamp, and hardly anything else in the room. Those buyers will pay real money for one good piece on a bare wall, and that's a big part of why the Japanese look fetches more than the farmhouse signs two tables over. I made a kumiko coaster too, just one coaster, 80 minutes, and every person who picked it up asked me where the other three were. The panel did earn its keep another way, though. It pulled people over to my table, and more than one of them walked off with a set of the charred coasters you'll meet later. So the precision piece finished last, beaten by builds a beginner can knock out in under an hour.
 
Number nine. The shoji-style lantern, and it only beat the panel by about 4 bucks an hour.
 
Shoji are the sliding doors in Japanese houses, a wood lattice with paper across it so the light comes through soft, and the old Japanese floor lamps called andon were built on that same idea. I made a tabletop version about a foot tall, out of two pine one-by-twos, the leftover screen molding from the panel, a roll of shoji paper, and a battery LED puck light tucked in the bottom. The little grid on each side uses the same notch trick as the panel, just with far fewer crossings, so it goes a whole lot faster.
 
And I do mean a battery light. Paper and dry pine will catch if a candle tips over or burns down low, so an LED is the only thing that goes inside one of these, whether you keep it or sell it, and you tell your buyer that same thing when you hand it over.
 
The frame went together easy, four corner posts and a few rails, glued and clamped square. The paper is where I lost my patience, twice. The first sheet tore because I yanked it tight while the glue was still wet. On the second one I got scared of tearing it, so it sagged like a hammock. What finally worked was gluing the paper down, letting the glue grab for a few minutes, then misting the paper with a spray bottle, because shoji paper shrinks a little as it dries and pulls itself tight as a drum. That's an old shoji trick, and it works on the cheap paper too. And if a side ever tears after somebody buys it, they can peel it off and paper it again, which is a nice thing to be able to tell a buyer.
 
Counting just what went into this one lantern, it came to about $15. It took 210 minutes. They sell for $65 to $90, mostly to people who want a soft light next to the bed, or a gift for somebody who's crazy about anything Japanese. Take your listing photo with the lantern lit and the room dark, because that glow is what they're paying for. That comes to about $18 an hour, for the prettiest thing I made all month.
 
So the two fanciest builds I made are sitting at the bottom of this list, and number one is still hiding in that coffee can of scraps.

Number eight. The joinery display shelf, which climbed about $8 an hour past the lantern without a single screw holding it together.
 
Japanese carpenters are famous for joints that lock wood together with no nails or screws, and plenty of those joints take a long apprenticeship to get right. I went with the friendliest one there is, the half-lap, where you cut away half the thickness of two boards so they cross and sit flush. To mark one, lay the crossing board right on top and run a sharp pencil down both edges, so the notch comes out exactly as wide as the board that drops into it. The shelf board is a one-by-six, the uprights are one-by-threes, and every joint gets a quarter-inch dowel driven through it as a peg. And I left every joint showing on purpose, because on this style of shelf, the joint is the decoration. Use an oak dowel, and the pegs come out a shade darker than the pine, so every joint stands out like you meant it to. People who buy this style want to see how a thing was put together, and a pegged joint shows them without you saying a word.
 
I cut all of it with the same pull saw from the lattice panel. A Japanese pull saw cuts when you pull it toward you, which keeps that thin blade from buckling, and a decent one runs about $30 at the box store. Some folks swear by their push saws, and they're welcome to them. I think a pull saw has a beginner sawing straighter by the end of one afternoon, and I'll stand by that one.
 
The chisel is where this shelf ate my afternoon. Every lap has to come out flat on the bottom, so you saw a few cuts across the waste, knock the chunks out, and pare it flat with the chisel, same rule as before, board clamped down and both hands behind the edge. When you hang it, screw a hidden cleat on the back into a wall stud, because a loaded shelf hanging on drywall alone will come down sooner or later, and it always picks a bad time.
 
Materials ran about $13 for the pine and the dowel. It took 165 minutes, and most of that was chisel work. It sells for $70 to $100 to folks who want a spot to set a little pottery or a plant where company can see those pegged joints. That works out to about $26 an hour. Still, the next one pulled ahead the minute I burned somebody's name into it.
 
Number seven. The tea tray, a plain, low serving tray, and it only moved up this list once I started putting names on it.
 
Japanese tea often comes out on a simple wooden tray, no carving and no curly handles, just clean edges and good-looking wood, and that plain look is exactly what sells it. Mine is a one-by-twelve pine board for the bottom, about 17 inches long, with one-by-two rails glued and pinned along the sides and taller one-by-four pieces on the ends. For the handholds, I drilled a hole at each end of a slot in those end pieces and cut out between the holes with a jigsaw. Then I rounded over every edge with sandpaper until it felt like a river rock in my hand, because a tray is something people pick up, and a sharp corner is what makes a piece look homemade.
 
This one carries food, so the torch stays off it completely, and the finish has to say food safe right on the label, like the plain mineral oil they sell for cutting boards.
 
The plain trays sold fine. But the ones with a family name or a wedding date burned into one of the end pieces with a cheap wood-burning pen went for the top of the range every single time, and they went first. Pencil the letters in and practice on a scrap before you touch the tray, because every wobble from a wood-burning pen stays right there in the wood. I took names at the market table and had the trays ready the next weekend, and nobody minded the wait. Put somebody's name on a thing and the price goes up, and this tray proved it all over again.
 
It's about $18 in wood, glue, and oil, 100 minutes of work, and it sells for $55 to $80, with the personalized ones sitting up at the top. Housewarming and wedding gift shoppers bought most of mine, and it averages out right around $30 an hour. Even so, it lost to a crowd I didn't know lived in my town.
 
Number six. The bonsai display stand, and it jumped almost $9 an hour over the tray.
 
Bonsai people put their best trees on low wooden stands, at shows and on their own shelves, and the stand counts as part of the display, a little like the frame on a painting. Folks spend years shaping a single tree, so paying for a nice stand to show it off doesn't bother them one bit. My version has a top just shy of a foot square, cut from a piece of one-by-twelve pine, with four short legs cut from a two-by-two and set in from the corners, so the top looks like it's floating. I glued each leg on, drove a screw down through the top into it, and plugged the screw hole with a bit of dowel sanded flush. Knock the edges down with a little chamfer and sand out every scratch you can find, because bonsai people look at a stand with their nose about touching the wood. Then finish it with a dark oil, so it sits back and lets the tree do the showing off.
 
The buyer is what surprised me. A fellow from a bonsai club bought three at once, one for each of his best trees, and a week later his club friends started messaging me. I had no idea there was a bonsai crowd around here, and those folks buy stands the way the rest of us buy extension cords. After that first order I built a longer version for the wide trees, and those went to the same bunch.
 
The wood cost about $9, it took 75 minutes, and they go for $45 to $70. If there's no bonsai club near you, a snake plant looks awfully good sitting on one too. Call it $39 an hour. That's five builds down, and not one of them has touched a flame yet. That changes right here, and my neighbor across the street watched the whole thing from his driveway.
 
Number five. Charred cedar planters, done the Japanese way called yakisugi, which you'll mostly hear called shou sugi ban over here.
 
For centuries, Japanese builders have charred the outside of cedar siding to help it stand up to weather, rot, and bugs, and the old way was to tie three boards into a tall triangle, like a chimney, and light a fire down inside it. Sugi is the Japanese cedar, and a cedar fence picket from the box store, about $4.50 apiece, takes the fire just fine. Two pickets make one box planter about a foot long. Cut your sides, screw the box together with exterior screws, drill a couple of drain holes in the bottom, and then carry it outside to burn.
 
And I mean outside, on concrete or gravel, well away from the house, the dry grass, and anything else that can catch. Keep a hose or a fire extinguisher within arm's reach, wear gloves and safety glasses, stand upwind of the smoke, and keep that torch pointed away from you and your feet. I keep a spray bottle of water right next to the torch for flare-ups, and Biscuit gets shut in the house, since that dog is convinced every board I burn is a barbecue.
 
Move the propane torch across the wood nice and slow, until the surface cracks into that alligator-skin look, then let it cool all the way down. Then scrub off the loose char with a wire brush. The more you brush, the more golden grain shows through, so you get to decide how dark it ends up. Wear a dust mask for the brushing, because that soot gets everywhere. Wipe it down and give it a coat of boiled linseed oil, and lay the oily rags out flat outside to dry, because rags soaked in that stuff can heat up and catch fire on their own in a wadded pile. Then drop a plastic nursery pot inside instead of filling it with dirt, so the wood stays drier and lasts a lot longer.
 
Before the torch, it's a box that looks like I threw it together in the driveway. After the torch and the brush, it looks like something from a fancy garden shop, and people can see that from across a parking lot. Every offcut from these went into a metal coffee can on the bench, and only after it was stone cold, because a warm ember in a can full of scraps is how you lose a garage. Hang onto that can.
 
Two pickets and a handful of screws come to about $11, it takes 50 minutes counting the burning and brushing, and they go for $38 to $60 to anybody with a porch, a patio, or a houseplant habit. That pays about $46 an hour, and the fire is doing most of the selling. The next one uses that same torch, plus the one drill bit I'm going to make you clamp down for.
 
Number four. The sake set tray, and it passed the planters by about $8 an hour.
 
A sake set is a small bottle and a few tiny cups, and people who own one like having something nice to set it all on for sushi night at home. Mine starts as an 18-inch piece of one-by-six pine with two little runners glued underneath, so it sits up off the table the way a lot of Japanese trays do, and those runners give your fingers somewhere to go when you pick it up. Then I cut shallow round pockets into the top with a Forstner bit, so every cup gets its own spot and nothing slides around. Pick a bit a hair wider than the bottom of the cups, and only go deep enough that a cup can't skate off.
 
Clamp that board to the bench before a Forstner bit ever touches it. That bit bites hard, and if it catches in a loose board, it'll spin the board right out of your hand. I also clamp a scrap with the hole already drilled in it on top as a guide, so the bit can't wander off your mark.
 
The torch only touches the sides and the underside. The top, where the cups sit, gets sanded smooth and wiped with the same food-safe oil as the tea tray. Cut the pockets a little bigger, and the same tray holds a small teapot and tea cups for the folks who'd rather have tea. Burn a couple's names into the end, and it turns into a wedding gift, which is where most of mine ended up.
 
The wood and the felt pads came to about $6, it took 45 minutes, and they sell for $38 to $55, which lands at about $54 an hour. And yet what's coming next emptied my table before anything else did.
 
Number three. Charred cedar coasters, sold in sets of four, and these were the first thing gone from my table.
 
One cedar picket gives you enough for three sets, with room to toss the knotty pieces. Cut it into 4-inch squares and sand the faces flat. Then lay the whole batch out on a scrap of cement board, since it won't burn, and char the entire row in one pass before you brush them just like the planters. The step people skip is the sealing. Give them a couple of good coats of a clear finish, then wipe one hard with a white paper towel, and if the towel comes back gray, keep sealing, because nobody wants soot on the bottom of their glass. Stick cork dots underneath, stack each set, and tie it with a little jute twine from the garden aisle, and it looks like a gift before anybody even asks.
 
A set costs me about $3 counting the cork and the finish, it takes 30 minutes a set when I batch them, and a set brings $28 to $36. They were gone before my thermos of coffee went cold. That's about $58 an hour, and they're the easiest yes on the whole table, because at that price somebody can buy a set without checking with anybody first. Every corner I trimmed off those squares went into the coffee can too, and by now it was getting full. The next one made more money per piece than anything else I built.
 
Number two. Minimalist wall art, charred cedar slats on a plain frame, and no single piece I made brought in more money.
 
This one's five cedar pickets cut into slats of different lengths. I burned some of them deep and brushed them hard, burned others light, and left one slat raw, so there's a stripe of bare cedar running through all that black. Then lay them out on the garage floor and shuffle them around until the lengths look random but balanced, with the raw one near the middle, and screw them to a one-by-two frame from behind so no screw heads show on the front. It pulls the same Japandi buyers as the lattice panel from number ten, except this one took a third of the time, and it doesn't care one bit if your saw cuts wander a little.
 
The hardwood crowd might wince at fence pickets hanging in somebody's living room, and I love them for caring that much, but not one buyer asked me what kind of wood it was. It's heavier than it looks, so hang it on a French cleat screwed into the wall studs. A single nail in drywall won't hold it for long.
 
Five pickets plus the frame run about $24, it takes 120 minutes, and it sells for $130 to $190, mostly to people staring at a big blank wall over the couch. That's about $68 an hour, which would've taken first place just about any other week. But the jump from number two to number one is the biggest jump on this whole list.
 
Number one. Charred cedar incense holders, made from the scraps in that coffee can.
 
By the time the planters, the coasters, and the wall art were done, that can was packed with burnt ends and corners, all cedar, all charred on at least one face. It was the end of a long day of burning, my hands smelled like a campfire, and that can was sitting right next to the trash bin. I was about to dump the whole thing when I thought, why not drill a hole in one and see? A stick incense holder is just a small block with a hole drilled at a slight angle, so the stick leans out over the wood and the ash lands on the board instead of your table.
 
Square up the scrap with a couple of saw cuts, give it a quick brush, drill the hole about halfway through with a small bit, oil it, and put a felt dot on the bottom. That's the whole build. It takes 12 minutes apiece, and the cost is the oil and the felt, call it 40 cents. Every one comes out a little different, because every scrap is a different shape, and buyers liked picking out their favorite. Now, charred wood isn't fireproof, whatever you've read online, so a lit stick never gets left alone in a room, and you tell your buyers the same thing.
 
They sell for $16 to $22, and I sold every one I had in a single afternoon to folks who like to burn incense while they read or do yoga, or who just want the house to smell nice. Selling out that fast told me my price was on the low side, so the next batch went out closer to the top of that range. When I added up the chalk tally, I did it twice, because 40 cents and 12 minutes selling for around $19 comes out to about $93 an hour. That's more than six times what the lattice panel paid me, out of a can of scraps I nearly threw away.
 
Now look back at the top five. The planters, the sake tray, the coasters, the wall art, and the incense holders all went through the torch, and the two slowest, fussiest builds sat at the very bottom. The Japanese look people paid me for wasn't the precision work. It was the fire, because once a piece is charred and brushed, a crooked saw line just looks like character, and nobody at the table is measuring. There's even a Japanese idea for that, wabi-sabi, finding the beauty in things that are a little rough and imperfect, and a beginner's charred box has plenty of both. And if kumiko grabs you the way it grabbed me, build it anyway for your own wall, just don't count on it to buy next month's pickets.
