Every fall, the robins that worked your lawn all summer pack up and head for Florida. The song at first light goes quiet, the grass sits empty in the mornings, and nobody sees another robin until spring. At least, that’s what just about everybody figures. Across a good part of the country, plenty of those robins never leave at all, and some will spend the whole winter within a short walk of your back door.

And that first robin of spring you’ll be so happy to see in March? There’s a fair chance it was around the whole time. I’m Walter, and if you keep an eye on the yard from the kitchen window, this one’s for you. We’re going through seven things about the robin that hardly anybody knows, and by the end you’ll know where your winter robins are hiding and how to coax a few of them into view.

Ask the folks who head out every winter for the Christmas Bird Count, the big holiday tally birders have kept going for more than a hundred years, and they’ll tell you a different story. Robins turn up on their lists in town after town across most of the country, snow or no snow.

Number one. Robins don’t all fly south. Across most of the lower forty-eight, you can find robins all winter long. The ones that nest up in Canada and across the far north do head south, so the robin you see in January could be a traveler from up north instead of the one that raised a family on your porch light.

And if you live along the Gulf Coast or down in Florida, winter can be when you see the most robins all year, since that’s where a lot of those northern birds end up. The funny part is that the robin’s own scientific name, the one printed in the field guides, ends in the word migratorius, which means just about what it sounds like.

Seems somebody should have checked with the robins first. If so many of them stay, then why does the yard feel so empty come November? It comes down to two small changes in the robin’s routine. Start with the singing. That bright song at first light, the one birders have long remembered as cheer up, cheerily, belongs to spring and summer.

It’s a male letting the neighborhood know this patch is his and he’s looking for a mate. Once nesting season is over, that job is done, and through the winter a robin hardly sings at all. Mostly you’ll hear a low, soft clucking, a bit like somebody tutting under their breath, and that’s easy to miss with the storm windows shut.
