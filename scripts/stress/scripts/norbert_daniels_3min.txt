But you can change what happens when somebody tries your front door, and the weak spot comes down to a small metal plate and two short screws, about $8 to $12 to replace.

When you lock your deadbolt, the bolt slides into a hole in the frame, and the flat piece of metal around that hole is the strike plate. The one that came with the lock is about the size of a matchbook, held on with two screws that are usually under an inch long. Those short screws only go into a board of soft pine. Behind that board there's a small gap, and behind the gap there's the stud, nailed into the rest of your house. The factory screws never get anywhere near it. So when somebody kicks that door, the pine splits, the plate goes with it, and the door opens without the lock ever being touched.

Here's the test. Take one of those screws out and lay it across a quarter. If it can't reach from one edge of the coin to the other, it's under an inch, and it's nowhere close to that stud.

In the hardware aisle, look for a door reinforcement strike. Heavy-gauge steel, longer than your old plate, with four to six screw holes, and 3-inch screws in the bag. Every one of those long screws goes through the plate, through the pine, across that gap, and into the stud.

Drill a pilot hole for each screw first, so the pine doesn't split. Then drive each screw until the plate's tight against the wood, and stop. If you keep cranking, it pulls the frame back into that gap, and now your deadbolt drags. Finish the last few turns by hand, and quit the moment it snugs up.

Rather not pick up a drill? A handyman can have this on in about half an hour, for roughly $50 to $80. Tell them this. I want this strike plate on my front door, 3-inch screws into the stud, snug and not cranked, and I want the deadbolt turning easy when you're done.

Anybody driving by still reads your house the same way. What they can't read from the street is a heavy steel plate with long screws in the stud behind it. The only way anybody finds that out is by trying the door.
