Two people paid the same fare, boarded the same flight, and sat in the same row. By the time the beverage cart had come and gone, one of them was sitting in an empty row closer to the front, holding the whole can of ginger ale, a second bag of pretzels, and a bottle of water for later.

The other was still in row fourteen with a few inches of soda over ice. He figures she must know somebody on the crew. She doesn't. What she knows is which questions flight attendants like hearing, and when to ask them. I'm Helena Hart, and I read the fine print so travelers like us can stop learning these things the hard way.

Today you're getting fourteen things flight attendants will quietly hand over for free, depending on the airline, the flight, and what's left in the galley, along with the exact words that get each one. None of them is an upgrade, and each of them starts with a single polite sentence.

Number one. The whole can. On a lot of U. S. airlines, the flight attendant pours your soda into a plastic cup packed with ice, fills it about halfway, and the rest of the can rolls off down the aisle on the cart. On many of those carriers, you can have the whole thing just by asking.

I figured that out much too late, after more flights than I care to count spent nursing a thimble of ginger ale. So when the cart reaches your row, speak up. "Could I keep the rest of the can, please?" Juice works the same way. If they're running short, they'll tell you, and you're no worse off for trying.

One more thing about drinks, because it catches good people off guard. On a U. S. airline, the only alcohol you're allowed to drink is alcohol the airline serves you, so the little bottles tucked in your carry-on stay closed. That's in the federal aviation rules, section one twenty-one point five seventy-five, and it doesn't care how good your intentions were.

That takes care of your thirst. Now for the rumble that shows up somewhere over the middle of the country.

Number two. A second snack. When the basket of pretzels and cookies comes down the aisle, you're welcome to take one. You're also welcome to ask for another, and the only real trick is when. Ask while the cart is still working its way back, and you're asking the crew to guess whether the last few rows will come up short.

So wait until everyone's been served, then catch a flight attendant as they pass, or step back to the galley, and phrase it as an offer.
