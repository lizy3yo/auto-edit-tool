Put two ribbed hats side by side, one knitted on a pair of needles and one made on a ring of 41 plastic pegs, and on a cold morning your ears couldn't tell you which one they're under. They're made of the very same stitches, the knit and the purl. But only one of them needed the knitter's fingers to hang on to every single stitch, start to finish. The other one had pegs doing that job. That's the hat you're making today, and you won't touch a knitting needle once.

Hello, friend, and welcome to Pearl Parker. I'm Pearl, and this lesson is for you if the needles already beat you once, if your fingers have gotten stiff and don't care for fiddly work anymore, or if there's a child in your life you'd love to teach. Five steps will take you from an empty loom to a warm ribbed hat, gathered at the top and ready to wear.

The first step is choosing your loom, and on a round loom, that one choice decides the size of your hat. The inexpensive sets of four round plastic looms you'll find at a craft store are all you need, and each loom in the set makes a different size. Count the pegs on the biggest one. On a lot of sets it has 40 or 41, and that's the loom for a grown-up's hat. The next one down, with about 36 pegs, fits a teenager or a smaller adult head. The one with around 30 pegs is for a child, and the little one, with about two dozen, is for a baby.

And how many stitches do you cast on? You don't have to work that out at all. Every peg on a round loom gets a loop, so the number of pegs is your number of stitches, and the loom has done that bit of arithmetic before you've even opened the box.

If the head you're knitting for falls between two looms, pick the bigger one. The ribbed brim we'll make in step three pulls a roomy hat in snug around the forehead.

Then there's your yarn, and this is where a lot of first loom hats go wrong before a single peg is wrapped. The pegs on these looms sit a good way apart, so they want thick yarn. Look on the yarn label for the small picture of a skein with a number inside it. Those numbers come from the Craft Yarn Council's standard yarn weights, and many yarn companies print them. A 5 means bulky, which some labels call chunky. A 6 means super bulky. Either one makes a lovely, warm hat on these looms. For a first hat, pick a smooth yarn in a light or bright color, because you'll be watching your loops closely, and fuzz or a very dark shade makes them harder to see.

What if the yarn in your basket is worsted weight, the everyday medium yarn with a 4 on the label? One strand of worsted on these pegs knits up into something closer to a fishing net than a hat. You'd see daylight straight through it. So we hold it double, which means knitting with two strands together and treating them as if they were one thick strand.

And you don't need to buy a second skein to do that. A store-bought skein usually has two ends, one on the outside and one tucked away in the middle. Push a finger into the hole at one end of the skein and feel around until you find the inside end. Draw it out. Take the outside end as well, and lay the two side by side. Now you're knitting from both ends of the same skein, and the two strands will travel together the whole way.

Last of all, find the loom hook. It's the small tool that came in the box with your looms, a handle with a thin pick on the end and a little bend at its tip. Add a pair of scissors and a tape measure, and that's everything this hat asks for.

I want to stick up for the loom for a minute, because it gets underestimated. Maybe you've been told, or you've told yourself, that knitting takes quick, nimble fingers. What it really takes is something to hold your stitches while you make the next one, and on a loom, the pegs do all of that holding for you. Your hands work on one peg, and every other stitch stays put until you come back around to it. That's what makes a loom the right door into knitting for a child, for hands that arthritis has made stiff, and for anyone the needles already beat once. And what comes off those pegs is real knitting. By the end of step three, you'll have made both of the stitches all knitting is built from.

So before a single peg gets wrapped, check your table. Your loom is resting in your lap, or flat on the table in front of you, with its pegs pointing up at the ceiling. Your yarn sits beside it, either one thick strand or two worsted strands lying together. Your hook, your scissors and your tape measure are within reach. And every peg on that loom is bare. If that's what you've got, you're ready.

Step two dresses every one of those bare pegs in a loop of yarn, and it begins at the one peg that sits apart from the others. A round loom usually has one small peg sticking out from the side of the ring, away from all the rest. That's the anchor peg. Its only job is to hold the end of your yarn still while you get going. Tie your yarn end around it, leaving a tail about as long as your hand. A slip knot, the adjustable loop knitters traditionally start with, is the usual choice here, but any snug knot that won't slide off will do. And if your loom doesn't have an anchor peg, hold the tail against the side of the loom with your thumb until the first few pegs are wrapped.

Hold the loom however feels comfortable. If your hands tire of holding it up, set it flat on the table and turn it toward you as you work.

Now bring the yarn inside the loom. We're going to travel around the loom to the right, starting with the peg just to the right of the anchor peg.

This is the e-wrap cast on, which means giving every peg one full circle of yarn that crosses over itself. Take the yarn around that first peg clockwise, the same way a clock's hands turn. It goes from the inside of the loom, around the outside of the peg, and back to the inside, where it crosses over the yarn it started from. Pull it snug so it hugs the peg. Then check that you could still slide the tip of your hook in underneath it. That's the tension you want for the whole hat, a wrap that hugs its peg without squeezing it.

Now the next peg to the right. Inside, around the outside, back in, and cross over. Then the next peg. Keep going all the way around the loom, turning it as you go, until you've come back around to the anchor peg.

How can you tell a wrap is right? Look at each peg from inside the loom. The yarn should cross over itself there in a small X. If a peg has a plain loop with no X, the yarn only went partway around, so unwind that one and wrap it again.

Does it matter which way you go? Hardly at all. If you've seen somebody wrap the other way around, that's fine too, as long as every peg gets wrapped the same way as the one before it.

Every peg is wearing one loop now. Run your fingers around the ring and push each loop down to the bottom of its peg. That's your cast on finished.

The next round is where your first knitting happens. Do you need to join anything to knit in a circle, the way you would on needles? No. When a round ends, you carry straight on to the first peg again, and the circle joins itself. So go back to the first peg, the one beside the anchor, and wrap it again the same way, clockwise, so it's wearing two loops, one down low and one above it. Pick up your hook and hold it the way you'd hold a pencil. Slide the tip in under the bottom loop. Lift that bottom loop up, over the top loop, and off the top of the peg. Let go of it, and it drops down toward the middle of the loom. One loop is left on the peg, the one you just wrapped.

I call that the leapfrog, because the bottom loop jumps clean over the top one and lands on the far side. Wrap, then leapfrog, and you've made a knit stitch.

Now the next peg. Wrap it, and leapfrog it. Then the one after that, wrap and leapfrog. That's the whole rhythm of this round.

Finish each peg, wrap and leapfrog, before you move on to the next one. If you wrap the whole round first and come back to leapfrog later, the early wraps have time to go slack, and when a wrap goes slack, loops start jumping off their pegs.

What does that look like? A loose top loop can get dragged up along with the leapfrog, and suddenly there's a bare peg with a loop hanging just below it. If that happens, stop, and don't tug on your yarn. Slide your hook into that loop from underneath. Lift it back up and over the top of its empty peg. Push it down. An empty peg is easy to spot, so you'll nearly always catch a loop that's jumped off before it has a chance to come undone.

If you're holding worsted double, keep an eye out for one strand getting left behind after a leapfrog. You'll see a single thin strand sitting on the peg on its own. Lift it over too, so it follows its partner.

When you've finished the round, push every loop back down to the bottom of its peg. Make that a habit at the end of every round, because a loop riding high on its peg is the one most likely to jump.

Take a look at your loom now. There's one loop on every peg, snug and pushed down low. Peek down inside the loom, and you'll find a neat edge of knitting hanging there, with the tail from your anchor knot beside it. That edge is the very bottom of your hat. If one or two of those loops look a bit baggy, don't fuss over them. They even themselves out as the hat grows.

Next comes the brim, and with it the only brand-new move in this whole hat. It's how a loom makes a purl, and it's what lets your brim hug a forehead as snugly as any brim knitted on needles.

Step three puts a purl on every other peg, and that's the one change to the loom you had a minute ago. Knitting one stitch and purling the next, over and over, is called ribbing, which is the stretchy, ridged band you'll find around the bottom of nearly every hat and the cuff of nearly every sweater. The knit stitches stand up in raised columns, with the purl stitches sunk in between them. That in and out is what lets a brim stretch over your head and draw back in snug.

You already know how to knit on a loom. It's your wrap and leapfrog. So only the purl is new, and I call it the scoop, because that's what your hook does. It scoops a fresh loop of yarn up through the old one.

Start the round the way you already know. On the first peg, wrap and leapfrog. That's your knit.

Now the second peg gets the scoop, and we'll go slowly. Bring the yarn across the inside of that peg, so it lies underneath the loop that's already sitting there. Push your hook down through that loop, from the top, so the tip comes out below it. Catch the yarn with the tip of the hook. Draw a small loop of yarn up through the old loop. Keep that new loop on your hook. Lift the old loop up and off the top of the peg. Slip the new loop onto the empty peg. Then give your yarn a gentle tug to snug it down.

That's a purl. Look at the bottom of that peg, and you'll see a small bump, a little bar of yarn lying across the base of the peg underneath its loop, like a collar. That collar is how you'll always know a purl peg when you see one.

What if the new loop slips off your hook halfway through? Nothing's lost. The old loop stays on its peg until you lift it, so catch the yarn again and draw up a fresh loop.

The third peg gets a knit. After a scoop your yarn is sitting down low, so bring it back up, then wrap and leapfrog as usual. The fourth peg gets the scoop. The fifth, wrap and leapfrog. The sixth, the scoop. Knit, purl, knit, purl, to the end of the round.

How do you know which peg gets which on the next round? The pegs tell you. Every purl peg has that collar at its base, and the knit pegs have nothing underneath their loops. Each peg gets whatever it had last round, every round. So there's nothing to memorize, as long as you look at each peg before you work it.

If your loom has an odd number of pegs, 41 say, the start and the end of each round meet in a funny spot. Your last peg of the round is a knit, and the first peg of the next round is a knit too, so two knits sit side by side there. That's supposed to happen. It makes one slightly wider column in your ribbing, and when you wear the hat, you turn that column to the back.

And if you ever find two smooth loops side by side anywhere else, one of them should have had the scoop. Don't undo anything. Give that peg its scoop on the next round and carry on. One odd stitch in a brim won't show once the hat is on somebody's head.

Keep going, round after round, until the brim is about 2 inches deep. To check it, reach into the loom, draw the knitting gently down so it hangs straight, and hold your tape measure against it from the bottom edge up to the pegs.

If the scoop feels clumsy for the first several pegs, that's how it's meant to feel. By the second or third round, you'll probably find your hands doing the whole scoop without you thinking through each part.

Stop and look at what you've made. A band of ribbing about 2 inches deep hangs down through the middle of the loom. Around the top, every peg holds either a smooth loop or a loop with a collar under it. Pinch a bit of that ribbing and give it a stretch. Feel it spring back. That spring is what's going to keep your hat on your head in a wind.

Step four takes the collars away. From here on, every peg gets a knit, round after round, until your hat is tall enough.

You can keep doing the e-wrap you already know. It's the easiest wrap there is, and it makes stitches with a small twist in them, so the fabric looks like rows of tiny crossed V's.

Or you can try a second wrap, the u-wrap, which just means wrapping the peg in a U shape instead of a full circle. It makes flat, tidy V's, the same ones you'd see on the front of a store-bought sweater.

To make one, your yarn starts inside the loom, as always. Take it around the outside of the peg and bring it back in, so it sits just above the loop that's already there. This time the yarn doesn't cross over itself, so it cups the back of the peg in a U.

Then leapfrog, straight away. Hook under the bottom loop, up and over the new one, and off the peg.

Why straight away? A u-wrap has no crossing to hold it on, so if you leave it sitting while you wrap the next peg, it can slide right off. With the u-wrap it's always the same order. Wrap one peg, leapfrog that peg, then move along.

Keep that U a touch loose, too. If you pull it tight around the peg, lifting the bottom loop over it turns into a struggle. The tip of your hook should slide in underneath without any fuss.

So which wrap should you use? Both make a warm hat, so pick the one your hands enjoy. The u-wrap is the one to try if you'd like your hat to look as much like needle knitting as it can.

This is the long stretch of the hat, and it's where most of your time goes. A first hat usually takes two or three hours of wrapping and leapfrogging all told, and nobody is timing you. Do a few rounds with a cup of tea, set the loom on the side table, and come back to it tomorrow evening.

Does it matter where you stop? Not at all. Stop at any peg you like, and when you pick the loom back up, carry on with the next peg along.

Keep up that habit from step two, and push your loops down at the end of every round.

What if your yarn runs out before the hat is tall enough? Tie the new yarn onto the end of the old one with a simple knot, leave a few inches of tail on each, and keep wrapping. Those tails get woven away on the inside at the very end, along with the others.

After a while, the knitting hanging through your loom will look like a long, soft tube, rather like the sleeve off a sweater, and not much like a hat at all. That changes at the very end, with one long pull.

How tall should the tube be? For a grown-up's head, 8 inches, measured from the bottom edge of the brim up to the pegs. If you'd like it slouchy, add an inch or two. For a child or a baby it'll be shorter, and the easiest guide is a hat that already fits them. Measure that hat from its bottom edge up to the top of its crown.

Whatever the number, measure before you close the top. Once the top is gathered shut, there's no adding a few more rounds without undoing the whole finish. The first loom hat I ever closed, I closed on a guess. It sat on my head like a thimble on a thumb, and I had to pick the entire top out again to make it longer. So draw the knitting down through the loom, let it hang straight, and lay your tape measure against it. If it comes up short, knit a few more rounds and measure again.

By now your loom should look like a hat that's lost its top. There's a tube of knitting 8 inches long hanging through it, ribbing at the bottom and smooth knitting above, and one loop still sitting on every peg. Step five gives that hat its top.

Step five takes every one of those loops off its peg and gathers them onto a single strand of yarn, and that's how the tube turns into a hat.

First, cut your working yarn, which is the yarn running from your skein to the loom, and leave a long tail. How long? Wrap the yarn around the rim of your loom twice, and cut it there. If you're holding worsted double, cut both strands together and treat them as one. The tail has to reach all the way around the loom and still leave you plenty to tie off.

Now, before a single loop comes off its peg, that tail goes through every one of them. Why not lift the loops off first and thread them afterwards? Because a loop with nothing running through it can come undone and start a run down your hat, the way a snag runs down a pair of tights.

So go back to the peg beside the anchor. Push your hook down through the loop on that peg, from the top. Catch the very end of your tail with the tip of the hook. Pull the end up through the loop, and keep pulling until the whole tail has come through. The loop stays where it is, on its peg.

Then do the same on the next peg, and the one after it, peg by peg, until the tail has passed through the very last loop.

Before anything comes off, run your eyes around the loom and make sure the tail passes through a loop on every single peg. If the tail missed a loop, that loop could run once it's off its peg.

Now lift each loop off its peg, with the hook or with your fingers. They slide off onto the tail and hang there together like beads on a string. Untie your anchor knot too, and let that tail hang free.

Hold the hat in one hand and the tail in the other, and pull. Slowly and steadily. The loops bunch together, the top gathers in on itself like the neck of a drawstring bag, and the open end of your tube closes into a round, snug crown. It can take a few tugs while the loops slide together, so keep pulling until the hole at the top has closed up completely.

Use your hook to draw the tail through the center of that closed top, so it comes out on the inside of the hat. Turn the hat inside out. Tie the tail in a knot around one of the stitches near the center, pull it snug, and tie a second knot on top of the first.

Now weave the end away so it can't work loose. Slide your hook under a few stitches, catch the tail, and pull it back under them. Then go under a few more stitches heading off in a different direction, and pull it through again. Snip off whatever's left.

The tail from your anchor knot is still hanging at the bottom of the brim. Weave it away on the inside the same way, a few stitches one direction and a few stitches another, and snip it. Do the same with any tails from joining new yarn. Everything is tucked inside, where nobody will ever see it.

Turn the hat right side out, and pull it down over your head. The ribbing comes down snug around your forehead, and the top sits close and round where you gathered it. Every stitch in that hat is a knit or a purl, and on a cold morning, your ears won't have the faintest idea it came off a loom.
