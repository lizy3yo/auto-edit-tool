Out of 10 plain sewing projects I stitched up from cloth that was already sitting in our house, the one that paid me best for my time came from a stack of feed sacks my husband set aside for the burn barrel. But the one I was proudest of, a nine-patch crib quilt that took the better part of three evenings, finished dead last at about a dollar and thirty cents an hour. So we're counting all of them down, worst to best, with what each one cost me, how long it took, and what folks actually laid their money down for.

I'm Hannah Yoder, and this one's for anybody sitting by a window with a needle, a spool of thread, and a basket of worn-out work clothes, wondering whether plain sewing is worth carrying to a market stand. Folks will tell you that hand sewing takes too long to ever pay, that the machines do it cheaper, and after keeping careful count all summer, I can tell you just where that thinking is right and just where it is wrong.

Number ten. The nine-patch crib quilt, the one I was proudest of.

A nine-patch is nine little squares of cloth sewn into a block, and the blocks sewn into rows, and the rows sewn into a top, and it's about the first quilt any Plain girl learns to piece at her mother's elbow. Mine came from the good parts of two worn bedsheets and a blue work shirt that had gone thin at the elbows, all cut into squares no bigger than my palm, and pieced up by hand of an evening while the lamp burned.

Now I love a quilt, and there is nothing that sits on a bed in a farmhouse guest room like a hand-pieced one, but the counting doesn't lie. The chalk mark on my sewing room door says near enough to twelve hours, once you count the piecing, the layering with an old blanket for the batting, and the quilting stitches that hold it all together, every one of them put in by hand. The cloth cost me nothing, since it was already in the house, and the thread and a bit of binding came to about two dollars. It sells for fourteen to eighteen dollars at my stand, and people do stop and finger it, because a small quilt draws the eye the way nothing else does. But once you spread twelve hours across it, that's about a dollar and thirty cents for every hour I sat and stitched, and the hourly figure is how I'm ranking this whole list, because that's the number that tells you whether your evening earned its keep.

So who buys it? Young mothers setting up a nursery, and grandmothers looking for a gift to lay in a cradle, and now and again an English lady who wants a bit of handwork to hang over a rail. Those are good buyers, and I don't begrudge them one bit, but a crib quilt is a thing you make for love more than for wages. It did earn its keep another way, mind you. It pulled folks over to my table, and more than one of them, once they'd stopped, went home with a set of the little items you'll meet further down. So the piece I was proudest of finished dead last, beaten out by things a body can stitch up in an afternoon.

Number nine. The rag rug, braided from strips of old work trousers, and it beat the quilt by only a few cents an hour.

A braided rag rug is about the oldest way there is to use up cloth too worn for anything else, cut into long strips, braided in a three-strand plait, and coiled round and round and laced together as you go. Mine came from three pairs of my husband's work trousers gone through at the knee, and an old brown dress of mine besides, all torn into strips the width of two fingers.

The braiding goes easy enough, and I can do it with my hands while my eyes are somewhere else, but the lacing is where the hours pile up, because every coil has to be sewn tight to the one before it with a strong doubled thread, and you keep it flat on the floor as you work so it doesn't cup up into a bowl. I lost the better part of an evening the first time, letting it draw up tight, and had to pick out a good long stretch and start that round again.

The cloth was free, and the thread cost me maybe fifty cents. It took near enough to nine hours for a rug about two foot across. It sells for twelve to twenty dollars, mostly to folks who want something for a kitchen doorway or a spot in front of the wash sink. That comes to about a dollar and sixty cents an hour, for the sturdiest thing I made all season. Take it to the market on a rainy day and set it right by the door, and folks will step on it and feel how it lies flat, and that's what sells it.

So the two things that took me the longest are sitting right at the bottom of this list, and number one is still hiding out in that pile of feed sacks.

Number eight. The bread cloth, a plain square of cloth to lay over rising dough, and it climbed a good bit past the rug without a stitch of trouble.

A bread cloth is about the humblest thing on this whole list, just a square of soft cloth, hemmed all around, to cover a bowl of dough while it rises or to line a basket for the table. Mine came from an old bedsheet worn soft and thin from a hundred washings, which is exactly what you want, because a stiff new cloth won't drape over a bowl the way a soft old one does. I cut them square, turned the edge under twice so no raw thread shows, and hemmed them by hand with a small running stitch, which goes quick once your fingers know the way.

The cloth was free, and the thread was a few cents. It takes about twenty-five minutes to hem one nicely. They sell for four to six dollars at the stand, and I set a stack of them tied with a bit of twine, and they go to women setting up a kitchen and to folks who bake. That works out to about eleven dollars an hour, which surprised me plenty, seeing as it's the plainest thing I sew. Embroider a little wheat sheaf in one corner with a scrap of floss, and it goes to the top of that price every time, and those are the ones that leave the table first.

Number seven. The pot holders, sewn double thick with a bit of old blanket inside, and they moved up this list the minute I started quilting a pattern across them.

A pot holder is two squares of sturdy cloth with something thick between them to keep the heat off your hand, and mine came from the good backs of two flannel work shirts with a piece of a worn wool blanket tucked inside. You lay it all together, sew round the edge with the good sides facing, turn it right side out through a little gap, and then quilt a few lines across it to hold the middle from shifting, and tuck a loop of tape in one corner for hanging.

This one goes on a farmhouse kitchen wall by the cookstove, and that's just where I sell the most of them, to women who know a store-bought one wears through in a season. The plain ones sold fine. But the ones where I quilted a little diamond pattern across the face went for the top of the range and went first, because that stitching makes it look like more than a rag, which it is.

The cloth was free and the tape a few cents, so call it a dime for one pot holder. It takes about thirty-five minutes to make one carefully. They sell for five to eight dollars, and a set of two ties up nice with twine. That comes to around eleven dollars an hour as well, but the next one pulled ahead the moment I started stitching names on it.

Number six. The drawstring produce bags, sewn from the thin cotton of old bedsheets, and they jumped a good ways over the pot holders.

These are simple bags with a cord run through the top, for carrying home beans and peas and such from the market without the paper sacks. Mine came from bedsheets too far gone at the middle to sleep under but still good and strong at the edges, cut into rectangles, folded and sewn up two sides on the treadle, with a channel hemmed at the top and a bit of cotton cord threaded through and knotted.

The treadle is what makes these pay, because I can run up a whole seam in a minute where hand work would take me ten, and there's no strength lost in a good treadle seam. These sell right there at the market to the very folks who fill them, and the English shoppers took to them faster than I looked for, being that they don't like the plastic sacks anymore, and they'll pay for something they can use again and again.

The cloth was free, the cord about fifteen cents a bag. It takes about eighteen minutes to sew one on the treadle. They sell for three to five dollars, and folks buy them three and four at a time. Call it fourteen dollars an hour. Stamp a little leaf on the side with a bit of ink, and they'll pay the higher price and thank you for it. That's five projects down, and not one of them has emptied my table the way the next few did.

Number five. The feed sack aprons, cut from the printed cotton sacks the mill flour used to come in.

For as long as anyone can remember, the sacks that flour and feed came in were printed with little flowers and checks, so a woman could turn them into dresses and aprons once the feed was gone, and there's still a good stack of the old printed ones put by in most Plain houses, ours included. One good sack makes one full apron, cut with a bib and a skirt and long ties that come round and knot in the front, hemmed all round and sewn up on the treadle with a good strong seam at the waist.

Now these take a bit of doing, because an apron has to fit and hang right, with the bib the proper height and the ties long enough to reach round, so I keep a pattern cut from an old feed sack itself and lay it right on the cloth. Sew the ties on good and strong, because that's the first place an apron goes when it's pulled on and off all day at the wash line.

The cloth was already in the house, the thread a few cents, so call it a nickel for one apron. It takes about fifty minutes on the treadle. They sell for eighteen to twenty-eight dollars, mostly to English women who love the old printed cloth, and to Plain neighbors who wore out their last one. That works out to about twenty-eight dollars an hour, and the pretty old prints do most of the selling. The next one uses that same treadle and the plainest cloth I've got.

Number four. The zippered pouches, small bags for keeping needles and pins and such, and they passed the aprons by a good stretch.

A little pouch with a zip across the top is a handy thing to keep small goods in, and folks use them for everything from sewing notions to coins to the little bits a purse collects. Mine come from the sturdier scraps left over from the aprons and shirts, two squares of cloth with a zipper sewn in along the top edge and the sides run up on the treadle, and I line them with a thin bit of sheet so the seams don't show inside.

The one thing I buy for these is the zipper, and it's the setting of that zipper that takes the practice, so I sew it slow on the treadle with the foot right up close, and the first few I made puckered along the top till I learned to hold the cloth just so. Once your hands know it, one goes together in no time.

The cloth was free and the zipper about thirty cents. It takes about twenty minutes each. They sell for six to nine dollars at the roadside table, mostly to women who sew and to young girls who like a little bag of their own, and they go quick because it's an easy thing to pick up and buy. That lands at about twenty-two dollars an hour. And yet what came next cleared my table faster than anything.

Number three. The potholder loops, no, I'll say it plain, the hemmed dish towels from old bedsheets, and these were the first thing gone every market day.

A good dish towel is a plain rectangle of soft cloth, hemmed all round, and there's nothing a farmhouse kitchen goes through faster. Mine come from worn bedsheets, the softest and thirstiest cloth there is once it's been washed a hundred times, cut long and hemmed on the treadle down the sides with a small turned hem so no thread frays loose. I stitch a colored line across each end with a bit of thread from the basket, and that little stripe is what makes folks pick it up.

I run these up in batches on the treadle, a whole stack in an afternoon, and that's the secret of it, because doing one at a time you'd never make a wage, but hemming ten at a sitting the minutes fall away. Tie three together with twine and they look like a gift before anyone asks.

The cloth was free and the thread a few cents, so call it a nickel a towel. It takes about ten minutes each when I batch them. They sell for three to four dollars apiece, and folks buy them by the handful. They were gone before my coffee went cold at the church hall sale in the spring. That's about twenty-four dollars an hour, and it's the easiest yes on the whole table, because at that price a body doesn't have to think twice. The next one earned more per piece than anything I sewed.

Number two. The baby quilts, no, I've told you about quilts. Let me say the doll quilts and small pillows, the little stuffed pillows sewn from the prettiest scraps.

These are small square pillows, stuffed soft, sewn from the best bits of the printed feed sacks and the good corners of the work shirts, four little squares pieced on one side and a plain back, sewn up round the edge with a gap left to turn and fill, then stitched closed by hand. I fill them with the cotton batting scraps and the softest of the torn-up cloth, so nothing goes to waste at all.

Now these are quick, being small, and the piecing on the front is just a bit of practice that anybody could learn of an evening. They go to a hospital bedside as a comfort, and to a nursery for a crib, and I sell a good many to folks visiting the sick who want to carry in something soft and made by hand.

The cloth and the stuffing were all in the house, the thread a few cents, so call it a dime each. It takes about twenty-five minutes to make one. They sell for nine to fourteen dollars, mostly to folks wanting a little gift with some heart in it. That's about thirty dollars an hour, which would have taken the top spot near about any other week. But the jump from number two to number one is the biggest step on this whole list.

Number one. The feed sack tote bags, sewn from the sacks bound for the burn barrel.

By the time the aprons and the pouches and the pillows were done, my husband had a good stack of the heavier printed sacks set by that were too coarse for a dress but far too stout to burn, and there they sat next to the barrel. I was near about ready to let him have them for the fire when I thought, why not run up a market bag or two and see? A tote is just a big sturdy square folded over and sewn up two sides, with two straps of the same cloth sewn strong to the top, and there's no simpler thing to make on a treadle.

You cut the sack down to a good square, fold it, run up the sides, turn the top over twice for a firm edge, and sew the straps on with a good strong box of stitching so they can't pull loose under a load of vegetables. That's the whole of it. It takes about fifteen minutes on the treadle, and the cloth cost nothing, and the thread a few cents. Every one comes out a little different, because every sack has a different print, and folks liked picking their favorite off the pile.

They sell for twelve to sixteen dollars, and I sold every last one I brought to the roadside table in a single Saturday, to English folks who love the old sacks and to neighbors carrying home from the market. Selling out that fast told me my price was set too low, so the next batch went out nearer the top of that range. When I added up the chalk marks, I counted twice, because a few cents and fifteen minutes fetching around fourteen dollars comes to about fifty-five dollars an hour. That's more than forty times what the crib quilt paid me, out of a stack of sacks bound for the fire.

Now look back at the top of this list. The aprons, the pouches, the towels, the pillows, and the tote bags, near about all of them ran up on the treadle, quick and plain, and the two slowest, most careful pieces sat right at the bottom. What folks paid me best for wasn't the fine hand quilting I'm proudest of. It was the plain, useful thing sewn fast from cloth that cost me nothing, because a bag or a towel gets carried and used and wears out and gets bought again, where a quilt gets bought but once in a great while. There's an old saying we live by, that we use a thing up, wear it out, make it do, or do without, and every project on this list is just that saying with a needle in it. And if the fine quilting draws you the way it draws me, why, piece a quilt anyway for your own bed, just don't count on it to buy next month's thread.
