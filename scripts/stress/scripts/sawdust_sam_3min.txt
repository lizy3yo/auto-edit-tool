Go through an old mountain cabin looking for anything a store sold, and you will come up nearly empty-handed. A log restorer in Grayson County, Virginia, let me try it. The roof was split oak, the walls were hewn poplar, and the door hung on wooden pins with a string for a lock. But on the back wall hung an axe, a froe, and a drawknife, and the steel in those three was the one thing that homestead could not grow.

That steel is why the last skill on this list held the other twenty-four together. I am Sam, and this one is for anybody who has wondered how a family got through a mountain winter on a woodlot and very little cash. The title says lost, which is too strong, since folk schools still teach plenty of these and the Foxfire books recorded a good share, after north Georgia high school students began interviewing their elders in the nineteen sixties.

What faded was the everyday habit, so we will go from that cabin roof down to its fire.

Number one is the roof, split by hand from white oak or chestnut with a froe, and the direction of the split mattered most. They cut the log into short bolts, split each bolt into pie wedges, and rived the boards along those same lines, so every board runs from bark toward heart and stays flat in the weather.

They went up rough as shakes, or got shaved thin at one end with a drawknife to make shingles. The oldest cabins held long boards down with heavy poles laid across each course, so the roof went on without a nail. When you rive, the bolt sits in a fork or against a stump, never in your hand.

Number two squared the walls with a broadaxe. Cabin logs were usually hewn on two faces, inside and outside, and hewing did more than flatten them. It cut away the pale sapwood near the bark, the part that rots first and draws powder post beetles, and left darker heartwood facing the weather. They hewed green, right after felling, because a fresh log gives up its chips far easier than a seasoned one, and tall, straight tulip poplar was a favorite.

Green wood splits and shifts as it dries, so a new wall opens small cracks its first year. Keep your boots clear of the swing, and stop when your arms tire. The logs still had to lock together at the corners, and the notch they cut there handles rain in a way I did not believe until I saw it.
