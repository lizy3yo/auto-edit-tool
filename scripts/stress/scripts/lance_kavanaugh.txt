Somewhere in your house there's probably a kit. A bin in the hall closet, a bag in the garage, maybe a box under the bed. And there's a decent chance nobody has opened it since the day it got packed.
That's not a knock on you. That's how kits go. You buy the stuff, you feel better, you put it away, and a few years later it's a box of dead batteries and a flashlight somebody borrowed during a storm and never put back.
FEMA puts out a good list for what goes in that box. Water, food that keeps, a radio, a flashlight, extra batteries, a first aid kit. Start there. That's the floor. What we're doing today is what goes on top of the floor, the ten things the Navy way adds. And most of them aren't gear. They're the habits that keep the gear working when you need it.
I'm Lance Kavanaugh. This is for anybody who has a kit, or means to build one, and wants it to actually work on the bad night. Ten items, most of them cheap, a couple of them free.
Now, before the list, the job. A kit has one job. On the worst night of your year, when it's dark and you're tired and the phone isn't helping, it has to hand you what you need without you having to think about it. Again, without thinking. Tired people don't think well. That's not an insult. That's just people. The Navy worked that out a long time ago, which is why so much of how a ship runs gets written down, marked, and practiced until it's boring.
So we'll go through your kit the way your hands would actually touch it. The lid first, then the top pocket, your wrist, the bottom of the bag, the front flap, a spare corner, the car, and last, the calendar on your wall. Ten items. One at a time.
Number one. A checklist on the lid.
Tape a written list of everything in the kit to the inside of the lid, or to the outside of the bag if it's a bag. Every item, in the order it's packed, with a date next to anything that runs out. Water, food, batteries. The date it went in and the date you'll check it.
What makes a good one? Short. Print big enough that you can read it by a headlamp without squinting. And slipped into a plastic sheet protector, so a leaky bottle doesn't turn it into pulp. One page. If it runs to three pages, the kit's too big or the list is too fussy. Probably both.
The mistake people make is keeping the list on the phone, or in their head. The phone is the first thing that dies on a bad night, and your head is the second. The other mistake is writing the list once and never touching it again. You pulled the flashlight out during that storm last spring. It's in the kitchen drawer. The list still says flashlight. Right?
The Navy runs on checklists, and the reason isn't complicated. A checklist doesn't get tired, and it doesn't skip a line because it's sure it already did that one. People do.
So here's tonight. Open the kit and write down what is actually in it. Not what you remember putting in it. Those are two different lists more often than you'd think. Then pick when you'll check it. The rule of thumb a lot of people use is twice a year, when the clocks change. That's a good one, because the clocks do the remembering and you don't have to.
And if the list says flashlight and the kit says no flashlight, the list wins. Go find the flashlight.
That's the lid. Now, the thing that keeps that list honest.
Number two. A marker and a roll of tape.
A permanent marker and a roll of painter's tape or masking tape. That's it. Maybe the cheapest item on this whole list, and one of the most useful.
In the Navy, gear gets marked. It has a name on it, it has a place, and it goes back in that place every time. That's the whole idea here. Everything has a place, and you can find that place in the dark.
Here's how you use them. Every bottle of water and every can or pouch of food gets the date written on it the day it goes in. Batteries too. Then split the kit into a few smaller bags or pouches, and label each one by the job it does. Light. Chow. Paper. Clothes. Now when you reach in at two in the morning, you're grabbing the pouch that says light, not digging.
What makes a good marker? One that writes on plastic and doesn't smear the minute it gets damp. What makes good tape? Tape that comes off clean, so you can relabel without scraping.
The tape has a second job, too. If you ever have to leave the house, a strip of tape on the inside of the front door with a note on it tells whoever comes looking where you went and when. Gone to the shelter at the high school, left at three in the afternoon. That little note saves somebody a lot of worry.
The mistake people make is one big sack with everything loose inside it. A bag full of loose stuff is just a junk drawer with straps.
So tonight, sort it, bag it, label it. Ten minutes. Maybe fifteen if you're still looking for the flashlight.
Labels are on and everything has a home. Now reach for the top pocket, because that's where the next one lives, and it's up top for a reason.
Number three. A headlamp with a red setting.
A flashlight is on the FEMA list, and it should be. The Navy way adds a headlamp, and here's why. The night you actually need your kit, your hands are going to be busy. You're opening a door, carrying the bag, holding somebody's arm on the stairs, reading that checklist on the lid. A flashlight takes a hand. A headlamp doesn't.
Get one with a red setting. Ships at night have long used red light in the spaces where people need to see without lighting everything up. Your version is simpler. Red light lets you move around the house without waking everybody, and without lighting your windows up like a stage for anybody watching from the street. The most boring house on the street. That's still the goal with the power out.
What makes a good one? Big, simple buttons you can work with cold fingers. And if you can manage it, one that takes the same batteries as your radio, so the whole kit runs on one size. One size of battery is one less thing to get wrong.
The mistake people make is packing it with the batteries in. It gets bumped in the bag, clicks on, and runs itself dead in the dark of your closet for a week. Or the batteries sit in it for three years and leak. Either way, the night you need it, you're holding a very small paperweight. So store the batteries in a bag right beside it, or flip one around so it can't switch on.
And it goes in the top pocket. The first thing you need in the dark should never be at the bottom of the bag. Make sense?

Number four. A plain wristwatch.
A cheap, plain wristwatch. Battery or wind-up. Hands that glow a little in the dark, if you can find one like that.
Here's the problem it solves. On a bad night, the phone becomes your clock, and every time you check the time you're burning the battery you were saving for a call. A watch lets the phone stay off.
And time matters more than people expect. How long has the power been out? When did the radio say the next update was coming? When did you tell the neighbor you'd check on her again? Those are all clock questions.
Ships run on the clock. The day gets broken into watches, and there's a schedule that says who's awake, when, and for how long, so somebody is always alert and nobody's up for twenty hours straight. Your version is small. If there are two or more of you in the house, split the night. Somebody's up, somebody sleeps, and you swap at a time you both agreed on. If you live alone, the watch is how you pace yourself. Radio on the hour, phone off in between.
What makes a good one? It tells time. That's the whole feature list. The mistake people make is packing a watch that needs charging every night. A watch you have to charge is just a second phone, and now you've got two things dying instead of one.
So tonight, go look in that drawer. Most houses have an old watch sitting in one. Put a fresh battery in it, set it, and put it in the kit or on your wrist.
That's time. Now dig all the way down to the bottom of the bag, because the next one is the one people pack worst.
Number five. Socks and a shirt, rolled tight, in a zip bag.
A change of socks and a shirt, rolled up tight, sealed in a zip bag with the air pressed out.
The Navy teaches people to roll their clothes tight, and it's not just about looking sharp. A rolled shirt takes less room, it doesn't come undone when the bag gets tossed around, and when you open the bag you can see every piece at a glance instead of digging through a pile. It's a small thing. That's the point. Discipline is mostly small things, done the same way every time.
The zip bag is there because kits live in damp places. Garages, basements, the trunk of a car. And if you end up walking somewhere in the rain, the bag on your back is soaked long before you get where you're going. A dry pair of socks at the end of a long, wet night is about the cheapest comfort there is.
What makes a good pair? Wool or a synthetic, if you have it. Cotton holds on to water. Wool and synthetics let go of it a lot faster. And a shirt that fits you now, for the season you're most likely to need it.
The mistake people make is tossing in whatever old clothes they didn't want anymore. Then the kit gets opened three years later and the shirt is two sizes off and built for the wrong time of year. So check the clothes when you check the list. Clocks change, you swap the shirt. Easy.
Tonight, roll it, bag it, press the air out, and write the date on the bag with that marker from number two. See how these start stacking up?
Clothes are at the bottom, dry and flat. Now come back up to the front flap, because that's where the most important piece of paper in the whole kit goes.
Number six. A comms card.
An index card, handwritten, in a small plastic bag, or run through a laminator if you've got one. On it go the numbers you'd actually need if your phone was dead or lost. Family. A neighbor. Your landlord or your insurance company. The power company's outage line.
Here's a quick test. Say your kid's phone number out loud right now without looking. Or your closest friend's. A lot of people can't anymore. The phone remembers so we don't have to, right up until the phone isn't there.
Then add two more things. A place to meet, one near the house and one outside the neighborhood, in case the house isn't an option. And an out-of-area contact, somebody a few states away that everybody in the family checks in with. The usual advice is that when a whole area is calling at once, a call across the country can sometimes get through when a call across town won't. And most emergency folks will tell you a text often gets out when a call can't.
A ship doesn't wait for trouble to figure out how it's going to talk. The comms plan is set long before anybody needs it. Same thing here.
Now, one more piece, and it's the part most people skip. Pick a family safe word. One word everybody knows, so if somebody calls in a panic saying they're your grandson and they need help, you can ask for the word. Bad weeks bring out the scammers, and a familiar voice isn't proof of anything anymore. It doesn't take much recorded audio to fake one.
And the safe word does not go on the card. Everything else does. The word lives in your head and theirs, and nowhere else.
Tonight, write the card, and make the phone call to pick the word. Five minutes, maybe less.
Card's in the flap. Right behind it goes the thing you'll write in once the night actually starts.
Number seven. A small notebook and a pencil.
A pocket notebook and a pencil. Pencil, not pen. A pencil writes when it's cold, when the page is a little damp, and when you're pressing the notebook against a wall. It doesn't dry out in the bag, either. Put two in, or one and a little sharpener.
Ships keep a log. What happened, and when. It gets written down as it happens, not pieced together later from memory. Your version is the same idea, smaller. The time the power went out. What the radio said and when. Who called, and what they told you. When you left the house, if you left. What got damaged, if anything did.
That last part matters more than people think. When it's over, the insurance company will ask when it started. The landlord will ask. Somebody always asks, and a long night doesn't leave a clean memory behind. A few lines on paper will.
There's a second reason, too. Writing it down gets it out of your head. When you're holding six things in your head, you've got no room for the seventh.
What makes a good one? Small enough to live in the flap next to the comms card. Plain paper. Nothing fancy. A pencil has never once asked you for an update.
The mistake people make is figuring they'll just remember. You won't. Nobody does. Not on that kind of night.
So tonight, the notebook goes in with the pencil clipped to it, and the first page gets one line. The date you last checked the kit. Now the log's already started.
Paper's handled. The card for who to call, the notebook for what happened. That leaves one spare corner in the bag.
Number eight. A deck of cards.
A plain deck of cards, plastic-coated if you can find one, with a rubber band around it.
This sounds like the least serious item on the list. It isn't. Here's what nobody plans for. Most of an emergency is waiting. Waiting for the power, waiting for the road to open, waiting for the radio to say something new. Hours of it. And a long wait with nothing to do goes bad on people. Tempers get short. Kids get restless. You start checking the phone every few minutes, and there goes the battery you were saving.
Sailors have been passing slow hours with cards and board games about as long as there have been ships. Nobody thought of that as soft. It's just what keeps people steady through the long, quiet stretches.
If you live alone, this one counts double. A quiet house with the power out gets very quiet. Something to do with your hands helps.
What makes a good deck? One that survives getting damp and fits anywhere. That's all. We're not packing a game room here. One deck. Maybe a page in the notebook with the rules to a couple of games you'll forget.
The mistake people make is counting on the phone to be the entertainment. The phone has a job that night, and it isn't solitaire. And for what it's worth, a real deck of cards has never asked for your location.
Tonight, go find the deck. Check the drawer with the old watch. It's probably in there too.
That's the bag, top to bottom. Now pick it up and walk it out the door, because the next item doesn't live in the house at all.
Number nine. A smaller kit in the car.
A second, smaller kit that stays in the car.
Trouble doesn't check whether you're home first. It can find you at the gas station, on the drive back from the store, or halfway down a long empty road. I keep a small bag in my car for exactly that reason. It's not a copy of the house kit. It's the short version.
What goes in? Some water. A light. A copy of the comms card. A charging cable that actually fits the car and the phone you have now, not the phone you had two phones ago. A blanket. A little bag of rolled socks. That covers most bad afternoons.
Now, fit it to your place. Your drive is not my drive. If the store is two miles away on busy roads, keep it small. If you drive long stretches where you might not see another car for a while, it gets bigger. Again, fit it to your life, not to somebody else's list.
There are two mistakes here. The first is heat. A trunk in August is an oven with a license plate. Water and batteries don't love it, so the car kit gets checked more often than the house one. Every time you check the house kit, check the car too, and swap the water out if it's been baking.
The second is showing it off. A big tactical-looking bag on the back seat tells anybody walking past that there's something in that car worth taking. Keep it plain, keep it in the trunk or under a seat, and keep it quiet.
Tonight, build it out of spares. Most of what goes in the car kit is stuff you already doubled up on while doing the other eight.
Car's squared away. That's every place your kit lives. Which leaves the calendar, and the one I told you about back at number three.
Number ten. A drill date.
A date. On the calendar. Written in ink.
This is the one that decides whether the other nine are worth anything, and it costs nothing. Pick a quiet evening, a Sunday is good, and run the kit. Turn the lights off. Put the phone in another room. Pull the kit out and live out of it for a couple of hours.
Ships drill constantly. Fire, flooding, the whole crew to stations, again and again, on calm days when nothing is wrong. The point isn't that sailors don't know what to do. The point is that on the real day, it's the twentieth time, not the first. Nobody has to think. Their hands already know. Remember what we said at the top about tired people? This is the fix for that.
Here's what the drill will find. And it will find something. The headlamp batteries are dead after all. The car cable doesn't fit. The shirt's the wrong size. There's a number on the comms card nobody answers anymore. The marker walked off somewhere. Every one of those is a small, cheap thing to fix on a quiet Sunday, and a real problem to discover in the dark.
Time yourself, too. How long does it take to get from the couch to the front door with the bag in hand, the headlamp on, and the watch on your wrist? Write it in the notebook. Next time, beat it.
Now, your drill won't look like mine. Maybe it's just you and the house. Maybe it's you, your spouse, and a dog that hates the dark. Maybe it's getting a parent down a flight of stairs. Build the drill around who's actually in your house and what they actually need, and let the drill tell you what your kit is missing.
That's the Navy way in one line. You check it before you need it.
So pick the date, write it on the calendar tonight, and the other nine start earning their space in the bag.
