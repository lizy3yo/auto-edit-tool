Out of 10 scrap-quilting projects you can piece together from a bin of leftover fabric, the one that paid me best for my time came out of a coffee tin of two-inch squares I very nearly hauled out to the burn barrel. But the one I loved the most, a big sampler quilt that swallowed up three whole weeks of my evenings, finished dead last at about $4 an hour.

I'm Granny Ruth, and this one's for anybody sitting at a kitchen table with a straight-stitch machine, a rotary cutter, and a mountain of scraps too pretty to throw out, wondering if all that piecing is really worth anything on a market table.

Start with the one that lost. A sampler quilt is where you make a bunch of different blocks, a churn dash here, a bear paw there, a flying geese row across the middle, all the classic patterns stitched into one big top. Mine was lap-sized, twelve blocks, each one a different pattern, quilted with straight lines on my little machine because that's the only kind of quilting my machine and my back agree on. It took three weeks of evenings, and it paid me about $4 an hour.

Now the winner. Scrappy fabric bowls, made from the squares in that coffee tin. By the time the potholders and the coasters were done, that tin was packed tight with two-inch squares and little trimmed corners, and it was sitting right next to the wastebasket. I was about to dump the whole thing out when I thought, why not sew a few together and coil them up and see what happens?

Sew your scraps into one long strip, fold it around a bit of clothesline cord, and zigzag stitch as you coil it up, pulling it into a bowl shape as you go. That's the whole thing, no pattern, no measuring. It takes about twenty-five minutes apiece, and the cost is the cord and a little thread, call it fifty cents. Every one comes out a little different, and folks loved picking out the one that spoke to them. These bowls are for keys and candy, not for hot food or the microwave.

They sell for $16 to $24, and I sold every one I had in a single afternoon. Fifty cents and twenty-five minutes selling for around $20 comes out to about $47 an hour. That's more than ten times what the sampler paid me, out of a tin of squares I nearly threw out.

Keep all ten fingers away from that rotary cutter. I'll see you back at the machine.
