Out of 10 scrap-quilting projects you can piece together from a bin of leftover fabric, the one that paid me best for my time came out of a coffee tin of two-inch squares I very nearly hauled out to the burn barrel. But the one I loved the most, a big sampler quilt that swallowed up three whole weeks of my evenings, finished dead last at about $4 an hour. So we're counting all of them down, worst to best, with what each one cost me, how long it took, and what folks actually handed over for it.

I'm Granny Ruth, and this one's for anybody sitting at a kitchen table with a straight-stitch machine, a rotary cutter, and a mountain of scraps too pretty to throw out, wondering if all that piecing is really worth anything on a market table. Folks will tell you a quilt that sells takes a longarm machine, a design wall, and a fabric budget the size of a car payment, and after this little experiment, I can tell you exactly where that advice cost me money.

Number ten. The sampler quilt, the one I loved the most.

A sampler quilt is where you make a bunch of different blocks, a churn dash here, a bear paw there, a flying geese row across the middle, all the classic patterns stitched into one big top, and every block is a little lesson in itself. I'm not going to pretend mine belongs at a big judged show next to the ladies who hand-quilt feathers into every corner. What I made was a lap-sized top, twelve blocks, each one a different pattern, sashed together and quilted with straight lines on my little machine because that's the only kind of quilting my machine and my back agree on.

The fancy quilters buy yardage by the bolt and match every fabric to a paint chip, and matching a dozen blocks so they all play nice together is exactly where a beginner loses a whole month, so pace yourself and don't try to make it perfect the first time out. I dug into my scrap bin instead, but I'll be honest, a sampler eats scraps and then some, so I still bought about $30 of batting, backing, and a spool or two of thread. Every block has to come out the same size or the whole top puckers and waves like a flag, and I re-pieced more blocks than I'd like to admit in church.

This is where the whole thing ran away from me. I figured a week of evenings. The little notepad I keep by the machine says close to sixty hours, because between the piecing, the pinning, the ripping out, and the quilting itself, a sampler is really twelve small quilts pretending to be one. Materials came to about $40 counting the batting and backing. It sells for $180 to $240, and it stopped folks in their tracks at the show too, because everybody's grandma made one and they want to touch it. But once you take out the fabric, that's about $4 for every hour I sat at that machine, and hourly pay is how I'm ranking this whole list, because it's the number that decides whether your month of evenings was worth a thing.

So who buys it? Folks who want a real heirloom, the kind that ends up folded over the back of a rocker on a farmhouse porch and gets pointed at when the grandkids visit. Those buyers will pay good money for one keepsake, and that's a big part of why the sampler fetched more per piece than a lot of things on this list. I made a single sampler block into a little pillow too, one pillow, about two hours, and everybody who squeezed it asked if I had a whole quilt to match. The big sampler did earn its keep another way, though. It pulled folks over to my booth, and more than one of them walked off with a set of the little coaster mats you'll meet later. So the piece I poured my whole heart into finished last, beaten by things a beginner can whip up in an afternoon.

Number nine. The rag quilt, and it only beat the sampler by about three dollars an hour.

A rag quilt is the fuzzy kind where the seams show on the outside and you snip them so they fray into soft little ruffles after a wash, and it's the friendliest quilt there is for a beginner, because it hides every wobble. I made a baby-sized one out of flannel scraps, squares of batting cut a touch smaller, all sewn into little sandwiches and then joined with a fat half-inch seam that stays on the top side where you can get at it with your scissors. The fraying is what makes it, so you cut into every one of those seams every quarter inch, all the way around every square, and let me tell you, that snipping is where my thumb about gave out.

And I do mean flannel or cotton. Some slick fabrics won't fray no matter how you snip them, so test a scrap before you cut a whole quilt's worth, or you'll end up with stiff seams and a sore hand for nothing. Cotton and flannel fray like a dream, and the more you wash the quilt the softer it gets.

The sewing went quick, just squares and straight seams, no matching corners to fuss over. The snipping is where I lost my patience, and my kitchen shears too, because ordinary scissors will blister your hand halfway through. What finally saved me was a little spring-loaded pair of snips they sell in the notions aisle, the kind that open themselves back up so your hand just squeezes, and after that the fraying went a whole lot easier. And when a rag quilt gets grubby, the buyer just tosses it in the wash and it comes out fuzzier than before, which is a lovely thing to be able to tell a young mama.

Counting just this one quilt, it came to about $22 in flannel and batting. It took about twenty-eight hours with all that snipping. They sell for $70 to $95, mostly to folks looking for a soft, tough baby gift or something to keep in the car. Take your photo with it draped over the side of a crib in a sunny nursery, because that fuzzy, cozy look is what they're paying for. That comes to about $7 an hour, for the softest thing I made all season.

So the two quilts I put the most love into are sitting at the bottom of this list, and number one is still hiding in that coffee tin of squares.

Number eight. The table runner, which climbed about six dollars an hour past the rag quilt without a lick of quilting stress.

A table runner is that long, skinny quilted strip you lay down the middle of a table, and it's a fine way to use up strips too short for much of anything else. I went with a simple pieced design, just fabric strips of all different widths sewn side by side into a long band, the kind of thing they call a strip quilt, and it's about as forgiving as sewing gets. Sew your strips together, layer the top with a bit of batting and a backing, quilt a few straight lines right down the length of it, and bind the edges. And I leave the seams a little scrappy and mismatched on purpose, because on this style of runner, the jumble of prints is the whole charm. Folks who buy this want that homey, patched-together look, and a bunch of different scraps gives it to them without you saying a word.

I pieced the whole thing on my straight-stitch machine, same as everything else I make. A straight stitch is all you need for piecing, that's just sewing two edges together in a line, and it keeps a beginner sewing nice and even without wrestling a lot of fancy settings. Some ladies swear by their big fancy machines with three hundred stitches, and bless them, they can have them. I think a plain straight-stitch machine has a new quilter piecing a runner in one afternoon, and I'll stand by that.

The binding is where this runner ate my evening. Folding that skinny strip of fabric around every edge and stitching it down neat takes a little patience the first few times, so go slow, press it flat, and pin more than you think you need. When you give one as a gift, tell them it wipes clean with a damp cloth and washes gentle, because a runner lives on a table where somebody's going to set a coffee cup down sooner or later.

Materials ran about $8 for the batting, backing, and thread since the strips came out of the bin. It took about six hours. It sells for $35 to $50 to folks who want to dress up a plain table or set out something festive for the holidays. That works out to about $12 an hour. Still, the next one pulled ahead the minute I started putting names on it.

Number seven. The zippered pouch, a little quilted bag, and it only moved up this list once I started stitching folks' names inside.

A zippered pouch is a small padded bag with a zipper across the top, good for makeup, or knitting notions, or the little bits that rattle around the bottom of a purse. Mine starts as two scrap panels quilted to a bit of batting, folded and sewn with a zipper set in the top, and it's the kind of thing you can make from squares no bigger than your hand. For the quilting I just run straight lines corner to corner across each panel, and then round the bottom seams a touch so it stands up nice and holds its shape.

This one gets handled every single day, so I backstitch every seam good and tight, and I sew the zipper in twice, because a zipper that pulls loose is what turns a happy customer into a returned pouch. I also tuck a little folded tag inside the seam so a person can write on it.

The plain pouches sold just fine. But the ones where I embroidered a name or added a little tag inside went for the top of the range every single time, and they sold first. Take orders at the booth and practice your lettering on a scrap first, because a wobbly name stitched into a pouch stays right there for good. I took names at my church hall sale and had the pouches ready the next Sunday, and nobody minded waiting a week. Put somebody's name on a thing and the price goes right up, and this little pouch proved it all over again.

It's about $3 in the zipper, batting, and thread, about ninety minutes of work, and it sells for $18 to $28, with the personalized ones sitting up at the top. Gift shoppers and the young gals bought most of mine, and it averages out right around $16 an hour. Even so, it lost to a crowd I didn't even know was in my town.

Number six. The mug rug, and it jumped almost seven dollars an hour over the pouch.

A mug rug is like a little oversized coaster big enough to hold a mug and a cookie both, and folks keep one at their desk or their reading chair. It's about the smallest quilt you can make, just a few scraps pieced into a square about six by nine, layered, quilted with a couple of straight lines, and bound around the edge. Because it's so little, a person can use up their very prettiest scraps, the ones they've been hoarding because they're too special to cut into anything big, and that's exactly why these charm folks so.

The buyer is what surprised me. A lady from the local library reading group bought six at once, one for every gal in her book club, and a week later her club friends started messaging me. I had no idea there was a whole crowd of tea-and-a-book ladies around here, and those folks buy mug rugs the way the rest of us buy stamps. After that first order I made a slightly bigger one with a spot for a whole plate, and those went to the same bunch for their coffee mornings.

The scraps cost about nothing, the batting and binding maybe a dollar, and one takes about forty minutes when I make a batch. They go for $10 to $16. If there's no book club near you, they sell just as well at a farmers market table with the tea towels. Call it about $23 an hour. That's five projects down, and not one has come from the scrap tin yet. That changes right here, and my neighbor lady came right over the fence to watch me do it.

Number five. Crumb-pieced potholders, made the scrappiest way there is, where you just sew little bits together every which way.

Crumb piecing is where you take your teeniest scraps, the crumbs really, the pieces most folks throw away, and you sew them together edge to edge with no plan at all until you've built up a whole new piece of fabric. Two layers of that crumb fabric with a good thick batting called insulated batting in the middle makes a potholder that actually holds up to a hot pan. Sew your crumbs into a square about eight inches, quilt them down, layer with the heat batting, and bind the edge with a little loop to hang it by.

And I mean that insulated batting, the kind made to take the heat, not the fluffy stuff you'd put in a bed quilt, because a potholder that lets the heat through is a burned hand waiting to happen, and you tell your buyer that same thing when you hand it over. Sew it double and bind it tight so it lasts.

There's real freedom in crumb piecing, because there's no pattern to follow and no seams to match, so a beginner who's scared of getting it wrong can just relax and sew. Before you press it, it looks like a rag heap on the table. After you square it up and bind it, it looks like something out of a fancy farmhouse kitchen catalog, and folks can see that from clear across the booth. Every scrap too small even for crumbs went into a coffee tin on my sewing table. Hang onto that tin.

The crumbs cost nothing, the heat batting and binding come to about a dollar fifty a pair, and a pair takes about forty-five minutes together, and they sell for $14 to $22 to anybody with a stove and a plate of biscuits. That pays about $24 an hour, and the scraps are doing most of the selling. The next one uses that same crumb idea, plus a little trick for the corners I'm going to make you press flat.

Number four. The pieced placemat set, and it passed the potholders by about eight dollars an hour.

A placemat is just a small quilt sized to fit under a plate, and folks who like to set a nice table will buy a whole set of four or six at once, which is the beauty of it. Mine starts as scrap strips sewn into a rectangle about twelve by eighteen, layered with a thin batting so it lays flat on the table, quilted with straight lines, and bound around. I keep the batting thin on these, because a puffy placemat rocks a plate around and nobody wants their soup sliding.

Press every seam flat before you quilt it, because a bumpy seam under a thin placemat shows every lump once it's on the table. I press as I go, seam by seam, and it makes the whole thing lay down smooth and professional.

These wipe clean and wash easy, so I tell folks to just toss the whole set in the machine. Make them a touch bigger and the same set works as a place to set a hot casserole dish. Stitch a couple's initials into the corner of each one and it turns into a wedding gift, which is where most of mine ended up going.

The scraps, batting, and binding came to about $2 a set of four, it took about two hours a set, and they sell for $40 to $60, which lands at about $24 an hour. And yet what's coming next emptied my table before anything else did.

Number three. Little scrappy coaster mats, sold in sets of four, and these were the first thing gone from my table.

A coaster mat is a tiny quilted square about four inches across, pieced from a few scraps, quilted, and bound, and one bin of leftovers gives you enough for a dozen sets. Piece your squares, layer a bunch at once on a strip of batting, quilt them all in one go, then cut them apart and bind each one. The step folks skip is the binding, because it's fiddly on something so small, but a raw-edge coaster frays in the wash and looks homemade in the bad way, so bind them neat and they look like a real gift. Stack each set of four and tie it with a bit of ribbon or twine, and it looks store-bought before anybody asks.

A set costs me about a dollar counting the batting, binding, and thread, it takes about half an hour a set when I batch them, and a set brings $12 to $18. They were gone before my coffee went cold at the show. That's about $30 an hour, and they're the easiest yes on the whole table, because at that price somebody can buy a set without checking with their husband first. Every corner I trimmed off those little squares went into the coffee tin too, and by now it was getting full. The next one made more money per piece than anything else I stitched.

Number two. The scrappy throw quilt, big blocks of mismatched fabric on a simple design, and no single thing I made brought in more money.

This one's a lap-sized throw made from great big squares, maybe ten inches each, cut from all my biggest, boldest scraps and sewn together in a plain grid, no fussy little pieces, no points to match. I quilted it with simple straight lines running corner to corner and bound it with a scrap I liked. Then I laid it out on the bed and shuffled the squares around until the colors looked scattered but pleasing, dark next to light, busy next to calm, and stitched them up. It pulls the same folks who wanted the sampler from number ten, except this one took a fraction of the time, and it doesn't care one bit if a seam is off by a hair.

The heirloom quilters might sniff at big plain squares in somebody's living room, and I love them for caring that much, but not one buyer asked me how many pieces were in it. It's cozy and it drapes nice, so it sells to folks who want something real to curl up under. A throw like this ends up folded across the foot of a bed or draped over the arm of a couch in a den, and it earns its keep every single evening.

The batting, backing, and thread run about $28 since the tops came from scraps, it takes about eight hours, and it sells for $120 to $170, mostly to folks who want a real, honest quilt without paying heirloom prices. That's about $18 an hour, which would've taken first place just about any other week. But the jump from number two to number one is the biggest jump on this whole list.

Number one. Scrappy fabric bowls, made from the squares in that coffee tin.

By the time the potholders, the coasters, and the throw were done, that tin was packed tight with two-inch squares and little trimmed corners, all cotton, all too small for much of anything. It was the end of a long day of sewing, my back was hollering at me, and that tin was sitting right next to the wastebasket. I was about to dump the whole thing out when I thought, why not sew a few together and coil them up and see what happens? A fabric bowl is just a long strip of scraps wrapped around a bit of clothesline cord and stitched round and round in a spiral until it rises up into a little dish.

Sew your scraps into one long strip, fold it around the cord, and zigzag stitch as you coil it up, pulling it into a bowl shape as you go. That's the whole thing, no pattern, no measuring, no matching. It takes about twenty-five minutes apiece once you get the hang of the coiling, and the cost is the cord and a little thread, call it fifty cents. Every one comes out a little different, because every handful of scraps is different, and folks loved picking out the one that spoke to them. Now, these bowls are for keys and candy and spools of thread, not for hot food or the microwave, and you tell your buyers that same thing.

They sell for $16 to $24, and I sold every one I had in a single afternoon to folks who wanted a pretty catch-all for the entry table, or a spot by the kitchen sink for their rings. Selling out that fast told me my price was on the low side, so the next batch went out closer to the top of that range. When I added up my little notepad, I did the figuring twice, because fifty cents and twenty-five minutes selling for around $20 comes out to about $47 an hour. That's more than ten times what the sampler paid me, out of a tin of squares I nearly threw out.

Now look back at the top five. The potholders, the placemats, the coasters, the throw, and the fabric bowls all used up the humblest scraps I had, and the two slowest, fussiest quilts sat right there at the very bottom. The homemade quilt folks paid me best for wasn't the precision piecing. It was the scrappy, made-with-love look, because once a piece is bound up neat and pressed flat, a wobbly seam just looks like character, and nobody at the booth is measuring my points. There's an old saying among quilters that a quilt made with love keeps a body warmer than one made perfect, and a beginner's scrappy bowl has plenty of both. And if a sampler quilt grabs your heart the way it grabbed mine, stitch it anyway for your own porch rocker, just don't count on it to buy next month's batting.
