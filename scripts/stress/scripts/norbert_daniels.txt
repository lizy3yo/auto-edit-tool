That's the house somebody picks when they want in during the day without running into anyone. Nobody's going to tell you to buy a second car for show. A house that's easy to read from the street, and empty on a regular schedule, is the kind that gets tried in broad daylight.

Maybe the kids are grown and gone. Maybe you're on your own after a lot of years with somebody, or maybe you've always liked the quiet. Either way, when people in your spot start worrying about a break-in, they usually go shopping for a fancier lock or an alarm system. I'd start somewhere a lot cheaper, because when a door gets kicked in, the part that gives is usually the wood around the lock.

The piece I'm talking about lives on the door frame. When you lock your deadbolt, the bolt slides into a hole in the frame, and the flat piece of metal around that hole is called the strike plate. The one that came with the lock is about the size of a matchbook, maybe a little bigger, and it's held on with two screws.

Now think about everything that's holding that door shut when the deadbolt's locked. There's the bolt, which is solid steel and the strongest thing in the whole setup. There's that thin little plate. There are the two screws, which are usually under an inch long. And there's the frame itself, which on most houses is a board of soft pine not even an inch thick. Those short screws only go into that board. They're holding the plate onto the pine, and that's all they're doing.

Behind that board there's a small gap, and behind the gap there's a two-by-four standing up inside the wall. That's the stud, and it's nailed into the rest of your house. The factory screws never get anywhere near it.

When somebody kicks that door near the lock, the whole kick goes through the bolt, into that plate, and into one small patch of pine about the size of the plate. Pine splits along the grain, the same way a piece of kindling does, and with two short screws holding it, that patch lets go on the first good kick. A strip of the frame breaks away, the plate goes with it, and the door opens without the lock ever being touched.

So how do you tell whether the plate on your own front door is the factory one? You don't need a tape measure for this. You need a screwdriver and a quarter.

Open the door and look at the plate first. Matchbook size with two screws means it's what the builder put in. Then take one of those screws out and lay it across a quarter. A quarter's just under an inch across. If that screw can't reach from one edge of the coin to the other, it's under an inch, and it's nowhere close to that stud in the wall. Don't be surprised if it comes up short. Builders buy these screws by the box. Put it back in for now, since you're about to replace it anyway.

While you're standing there, hold a fridge magnet against the frame. If it sticks, your frame is steel. Everything in this video is for a wood frame, and steel takes different hardware, so that one's a call to a locksmith.

What does a better plate do that the factory one can't? The one you want is sold at hardware stores as a door reinforcement strike. It's heavy-gauge steel, a good bit thicker than the factory plate, and it's longer, with four to six screw holes instead of two. And it comes with screws 3 inches long.

Those two changes work together. The longer, thicker plate spreads a kick across a lot more of the frame, instead of one small patch around the hole. And every one of those long screws goes through the plate, through the pine, across that gap, and into the stud. Before that door moves now, a kick has to tear four to six long screws out of the framing of your house.

Nothing makes a wooden door kick-proof, and I won't pretend this does. But a door that used to give right away now makes somebody work at it, and working at it takes time and makes noise, which is what somebody trying a door in the middle of the afternoon doesn't want. And if you happen to be home, that time is how you get to the phone.

Putting it in is simple. But there's one spot in the job where a good plate can leave your door worse off than it started, and it's in the last couple of turns of each screw. I'll show you exactly where to stop.

In the door hardware aisle, the package might say door reinforcement strike, security strike, or heavy-duty strike plate. Pick one up. It should have some real weight to it, and it should be longer than your old one, with at least four screw holes. You want 3-inch screws in that bag, and if the ones inside are short, pick a different package or buy a handful of long wood screws to go with it, because the long screws are what do the work.

Two more things to match. Look at the corners on the plate you've got now. Some are rounded and some are square, and you want the new one to match, so it fits the recess, the shallow pocket cut into the wood that your old plate sits in. Some of these heavier plates drop right into that recess. Others are longer, and then the recess has to be cut longer with a chisel. If you'd rather skip the chisel work, find one the same size as your old plate. And match the finish, brass or nickel or bronze, if that matters to you. The door won't mind either way.

Here's an easy way to get it right the first time. Before you leave the house, take a picture of your old plate. At the store, you can hold that picture up next to what's on the shelf. And if somebody in an apron walks by, show them the picture. That's what they're there for.

So how hard is this to put in? If you've used a drill before, you can do this one. You'll need a drill, a small drill bit, a screwdriver, and something to prop the door open. A doorstop works, or a kitchen chair.

Take the two old screws out and lift the old plate off. Set the new plate into that recess and press on the corners. You want to make sure it sits flat and doesn't rock, and that the openings in it line up with the hole for the bolt, and the latch too if the plate covers both. If it rocks, or it sits up higher than the wood around it, stop there. The recess needs to be deeper or longer, and that's a good point to hand the job to somebody with a chisel.

If it sits flat, hold it in place and drill a pilot hole through each screw hole, straight into the frame, as deep as the bit will go. Use a bit that's thinner than the screws. The reason you drill first is that pine splits when you force a long screw into it, and a split frame is what we're trying to prevent.

Now the screws, and this is the spot I told you about. Drive each one until the head sits down flat and the plate's tight against the wood. Then stop. Remember that small gap behind the frame? If you keep cranking after the screw is snug, it starts pulling the pine back into that gap. The board bows in, the plate moves away from the door, and now your latch won't catch, or your deadbolt drags on the edge of its hole. That's how a good plate leaves you with a door that's harder to lock than before you started.

If you're driving them with the drill, stop just short and finish the last few turns by hand with the screwdriver. Your hand can feel the moment it snugs up, and that's the moment to quit turning. And if one of those long screws spins and spins and never tightens, back it out and stop. It didn't find solid wood behind the frame, and that's another good time to call somebody who can take the trim off and look.

If your latch has its own separate little plate, the one that clicks when the door shuts, give it the same treatment. Pilot holes, long screws, snug.

Then test it. Close the door the normal way and let the latch click in on its own, then turn the deadbolt. It should slide all the way over without you pushing or lifting on the door. Unlock it, step outside, and do it again with your key. From out there, your door looks just the way it did this morning. If the latch won't catch or the bolt drags, loosen the screws slightly, one at a time, and try the door after each one. If that doesn't sort it out, leave the new plate on and have a handyman shim the frame. That means tucking thin wood wedges into that gap behind it, so it can't pull in and the bolt slides easy again.

Once the front door's done, give the back door and the door from the garage into the house the same quarter test. A kick works on those the same way, and the fix is the same plate.
