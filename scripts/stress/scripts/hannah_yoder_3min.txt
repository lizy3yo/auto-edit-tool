I'm Hannah Yoder, and this one's for anybody sitting by a window with a needle, a spool of thread, and a basket of worn-out work clothes, wondering whether plain sewing is worth carrying to a market stand.

Start with the one that lost. A nine-patch is nine little squares of cloth sewn into a block, and the blocks sewn into rows, and it's about the first quilt any Plain girl learns to piece at her mother's elbow. Mine came from the good parts of two worn bedsheets and a blue work shirt gone thin at the elbows, all cut into squares no bigger than my palm and pieced by hand of an evening while the lamp burned. It took three evenings, and when I counted it up, it paid me about a dollar and thirty cents an hour.

Now the winner. The feed sack tote bags, sewn from the sacks bound for the burn barrel. My husband had a good stack of the heavier printed sacks set by, too coarse for a dress but far too stout to burn, and there they sat next to the barrel. I was near about ready to let him have them for the fire when I thought, why not run up a market bag or two and see?

You cut the sack down to a good square, fold it, run up the sides, turn the top over twice for a firm edge, and sew the straps on with a good strong box of stitching so they can't pull loose under a load of vegetables. That's the whole of it. It takes about fifteen minutes on the treadle, the cloth cost nothing, and the thread a few cents. Every one comes out a little different, because every sack has a different print.

They sell for twelve to sixteen dollars, and I sold every last one I brought to the roadside table in a single Saturday. A few cents and fifteen minutes fetching around fourteen dollars comes to about fifty-five dollars an hour. That's more than forty times what the crib quilt paid me, out of a stack of sacks bound for the fire.

Use it up, wear it out, and God keep you. I'll see you back at the window.
