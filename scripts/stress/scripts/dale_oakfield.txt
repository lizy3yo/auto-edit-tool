You finished a walnut and maple cutting board a few weeks back. The glue lines are tight, you sanded it through 220 grit, it's got three coats of oil on it, and it's still leaning against the wall in your garage. Maybe you listed it online and it sat there with a handful of views. Maybe you carried it to a Saturday market and watched people walk right past your table on their way to the kettle corn. That happens to everybody who sells what they build, and it's fixable, because the board usually isn't the problem. The place you tried to sell it just wasn't built for that piece. Today I'm ranking Etsy, craft fairs, and Facebook Marketplace by what each one actually does well. Hang on for number five, because it's the Etsy fee that can more than double what comes out of a single sale, and plenty of sellers don't notice it until the payout lands.
I'm Dale Oakfield, and this is my workshop. This one's for the woodworker with finished pieces piling up and no clear idea where they'll sell. By the end, you'll know which pieces go where, what each place takes out of your pocket, and how to run all three without losing every weekend to it.
Number one. The first tip on our list is to sort your finished pieces by size and price before you ever pick a place to sell them. Skip this, and you'll put the right piece in the wrong spot. I've watched a good man haul a dining bench to a craft fair, where it ate half his booth, and not one shopper was about to carry it back to a car parked three blocks away. I've also seen folks try to ship a coffee table across the country and lose most of the profit to the box. So make three piles. The first pile is smalls, the things that sell for about $10 to $40. That's coasters, Christmas ornaments, small cutting boards, and planters. The second pile is personalized mid-tier work in the $50 to $200 range. Engraved boards, house signs, keepsake boxes, anything with somebody's name or a wedding date burned into it. The third pile is furniture and large pieces, running $150 to $600. End tables, benches, bookcases, a blanket chest. Write those three piles on a piece of scrap plywood and hang it over the bench if you have to. Once every piece has a pile, half of the selling decision is already made for you.
The second item is a short one. Use Etsy for the thing it does best, which is reach. At a market, your customers are whoever happened to walk in that morning. On Etsy, your shop is open at two in the afternoon and two in the morning, and a buyer three states away, hunting for a board with her parents' last name on it, can find yours while you're sound asleep. That's why the personalized pile belongs on Etsy first. Those boards, signs, and boxes in the $50 to $200 range are exactly what people go searching for when they want a gift nobody else at the party will bring. The catch is competition. Type in engraved cutting board and you'll scroll past page after page of them, so the shop that gets the sale is the one with clean photos and titles that match what buyers type. We'll get to both. Set it up right, and your work sits in a store that never locks its doors, without you standing behind a table.
And before we're done with Etsy, I still owe you number five, the fee that can more than double what the site keeps from one sale.

On to tip number three. Add up every Etsy fee before you set a single price. Skip it, and you'll price a board at $60, sell it, and wonder why the deposit looks light. It costs 20 cents to list an item, and that listing renews every four months, or after each sale, for another 20 cents. When the item sells, Etsy takes a 6.5% transaction fee, and that's figured on the item price plus whatever you charged for shipping. Then comes payment processing, which for a US seller runs 3% plus 25 cents per order. Put those together on a $100 board, and roughly $10 comes off the top before you've bought a single stick of lumber for the next one. So work your price backward. Figure your lumber, your finish, your sandpaper, and your hours, then plan on about a tenth of the sale going to fees, and build that into the number from the start. Write that on the scrap plywood too. Priced that way, every sale pays you what you meant it to.
The fourth item is to build shipping and breakage into every Etsy price. A cutting board is dense and heavy, and its corners chip if the box takes one hard drop off a conveyor belt. I've seen the photo a customer sends of a board with a corner knocked clean off, and that one costs you the board, the postage, and a Saturday making the replacement. So before you list, pack one exactly the way you'll ship it and set it on a bathroom scale. Put cardboard corner guards on all four corners, wrap it in a layer of bubble wrap, and leave about 2 inches of padding between the board and every wall of the box. Then set your shipping charge off that real weight, never a guess. And remember that 6.5% transaction fee from number three also rides on what you charge for shipping, so the heavier the box, the bigger that fee grows. Pack it like it's going to get dropped. Do that, and a sale stays a sale.
Number five, the one I promised you. Know your Offsite Ads rate, and price your Etsy work so it can absorb it. Etsy runs ads for sellers' listings on other sites, places like Google and Facebook. If a shopper clicks one of those ads and buys from you within 30 days, that order carries an extra fee of 15%. Once your shop passes $10,000 in sales over a year, the rate drops to 12%, but at that point you're in the program for good and can't switch it off. Below that line, you can opt out in your shop settings. Now take that same $100 board. The regular fees were about $10. If the sale came through one of those ads, add another $15, or $12 for the bigger shops, and now somewhere around $22 to $25 is gone on one board. That adds up in a hurry. On a $15 set of coasters, a 15% hit takes a big bite out of a small profit, which is one more reason the little stuff tends to do better at a market. For your personalized mid-tier work, leave enough room in the price that an ad sale still pays you. Know the number going in, and it can't ambush your payout.
Moving to the sixth tip. Take your Etsy photos in daylight, and write your titles in the words buyers actually type. Online, the photo is the only thing a buyer gets to touch. A board shot under a yellow garage bulb looks orange and flat, and with competition that deep, a dull picture gets scrolled right past. Set the piece on a plain white or light gray background near a window, in the middle of the day, with the flash turned off. Take one shot straight on, one at an angle to show the thickness, one with a hand or a coffee mug beside it for scale, and one close shot of the engraving so the buyer can read every letter. Then write the title the way a person searches. Lead with the words someone would type into that search bar, like personalized walnut cutting board, engraved family name, wedding gift. Save the clever product names for your own notebook. A small maker who builds up to around 30 listings with decent photos can realistically see somewhere between $500 and $2,000 a month from an Etsy shop. It's worth one sunny afternoon. Good pictures and plain titles are what get your work seen in the first place.
Number seven takes us off the computer and out to the fairground. Take your smalls to craft fairs and farmers markets. Coasters, ornaments, and small boards are impulse buys. People decide on them because they're already holding one, and that moment is hard to recreate on a screen. Booth fees run anywhere from about $50 at a small-town market to $500 for a big holiday show. In exchange, you ship nothing, you get paid in cash or by card right on the spot, and you get something Etsy can't give you, which is watching real people react to your work. Bring a card reader that plugs into your phone and a cash box with $100 in small bills for change. Put the $10 to $40 items along the front edge of the table where hands reach first, and set the pricier boards behind them. Then pay attention. Notice which pieces get picked up and set back down, and which ones leave with somebody. One good Saturday market can gross $500 to $1,500, and by closing time you'll know more about what sells than a month of online listings could tell you. That's the fastest education in pricing you'll ever get.
That leads straight into number eight. Count the whole cost of a market day before you call it a win. Gross is the number that feels good in the truck on the drive home, and it tells you very little about what you actually made. Markets are seasonal, with the busy stretch mostly in the fall and the weeks before the holidays. Weather decides a lot, since a cold rain can empty the aisles by ten in the morning. And it's a full day of labor. You load the truck the night before, set up before the crowd arrives, stand behind the table all day, then tear it all down and unload again. So do the math honestly. Say you grossed $900 and paid $150 for the booth. That leaves $750, and before you've taken out materials, it has to cover something like 14 hours of your time. One safety note while we're here. If you run a pop-up canopy, weight or stake every single leg, because one gust of wind turns an unweighted canopy into a sail, and it'll take out your table and maybe the fellow in the next booth. Weight all four legs. Run the real numbers, and you'll know which shows are worth the Saturday and which ones aren't.
The ninth item is the one furniture builders need to hear. Sell your furniture and large pieces on Facebook Marketplace and in your local buy-and-sell groups. Shipping a bookcase eats the profit, and hauling one to a fair eats the booth. Marketplace charges nothing to list for local pickup, there's no box to pack, and the buyer comes to you with a truck. This is where your $150 to $600 pile belongs. Photograph the piece in a real room, next to a wall or a couch, so people can picture it in their own house. Put the measurements in the very first line, width, depth, and height in inches, because otherwise the first question you'll get is whether it fits. Name the wood species and the finish, and say plainly that it's solid wood if it is. Then post it in your town's buy-and-sell group too, since those folks live close enough to pick it up that same week. Big pieces go to buyers who already have a truck and a reason to drive over.
Number ten, and this is the price you pay for that free listing. Plan for haggling and no-shows before the first message comes in. Somebody will offer you half. Somebody else will promise to be there at six and never turn up. Decide your floor before you post, and list the piece about 10 to 15% above it, so there's room to come down and still land where you wanted. When a buyer asks to come by, confirm the morning of, then again an hour before. If they don't answer, hold the piece for the next person in line. For safety, move the piece out to your driveway or open garage before pickup so strangers aren't walking through your house, make sure someone else is home, and lift with two people and a moving blanket, because a solid oak bench weighs more than it looks. That part isn't optional. Handle it this way, and the hagglers and the flakes cost you a few minutes instead of your whole Saturday.
And now the bonus, which is how I put all of this together. Don't pick one. Run all three, and let each one carry its own lane. Smalls go to the markets, where they sell by the handful, and your best ones get listed on Etsy too, so they keep selling on a Tuesday night. The personalized mid-tier work lives on Etsy, where people are out looking for exactly that. Furniture and big pieces go on Marketplace, where local buyers show up with a truck and there's nothing to pack. If you're starting from zero this weekend, list your biggest piece on Marketplace first, since it costs nothing, then book one market for your smalls, and build your Etsy shop a few listings at a time as you get good photos. Every channel has a weak spot, whether that's the fees online, the weather at the fair, or the buyer who never shows, and the other two cover for it. So let each lane carry the pieces it was built for, and none of them has to carry your shop by itself.
