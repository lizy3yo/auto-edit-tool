Somewhere in your house there's probably a kit. A bin in the hall closet, a bag in the garage, maybe a box under the bed. And there's a decent chance nobody has opened it since the day it got packed.

I'm Lance Kavanaugh. This is for anybody who has a kit, or means to build one, and wants it to actually work on the bad night. FEMA's list is the floor. Today, four of the ten things the Navy way adds on top of it, and most of them aren't gear.

A kit has one job. On the worst night of your year, when it's dark and you're tired, it has to hand you what you need without you having to think about it. Tired people don't think well. That's not an insult. That's just people.

Number one. A checklist on the lid. Tape a written list of everything in the kit to the inside of the lid. Every item, in the order it's packed, with a date next to anything that runs out. Keep it on paper, not on the phone. The phone is the first thing that dies on a bad night, and your head is the second. Check it twice a year, when the clocks change, because the clocks do the remembering and you don't have to.

Number two. A headlamp with a red setting. The night you need your kit, your hands are going to be busy. A flashlight takes a hand. A headlamp doesn't. Red light lets you move around the house without waking everybody. Store the batteries in a bag right beside it, so it can't click on and run itself dead in the closet. And it goes in the top pocket.

Hang on for the last one. It costs nothing, and it decides whether the rest are worth anything.

Number three. A comms card. An index card, handwritten, in a small plastic bag, with the numbers you'd actually need if your phone was dead or lost.

Then pick a family safe word. One word everybody knows, so if somebody calls in a panic saying they're your grandson and they need help, you can ask for the word. A familiar voice isn't proof of anything anymore. And the safe word does not go on the card. The word lives in your head and theirs, and nowhere else.

Number four, the one I told you about. A drill date. A date. On the calendar. Written in ink. Pick a quiet evening, turn the lights off, put the phone in another room, and live out of the kit for a couple of hours.

Ships drill constantly, so that on the real day, it's the twentieth time, not the first. Here's what the drill will find. The headlamp batteries are dead after all. Every one of those is a small, cheap thing to fix on a quiet Sunday, and a real problem to discover in the dark.

That's the Navy way in one line. You check it before you need it.
